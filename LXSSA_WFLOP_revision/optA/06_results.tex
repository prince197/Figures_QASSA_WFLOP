The run-level tests in Table~\ref{tab:wtl} show where these differences come from. PSO, which forms the first phase of PSO-VNS, is the closest competitor: PSO-VNS is significantly better in \NWtlPSOW{} cases, worse in \NWtlPSOL{} and tied in \NWtlPSOT, and its mean wake loss over the 68 cases is \NLossPSOVNS\% against \NLossPSO\%. Fig.~\ref{fig:wakeloss} shows how the wake loss depends on the case. It grows with $N$, falls as the farm becomes larger, and is higher for the broader wind rose of Data Set~II. For $N\le3$, all methods stay within \NLossMaxSmallN\% of the wake-free objective, whereas for 15 turbines in the 1000-m farm (Data Set~I), the mean wake loss is \NLossBigIPSOVNS\% for PSO-VNS, \NLossBigIPSO\% for PSO, \NLossBigISSAVNS\% for SSA-VNS and \NLossBigISSA\% for SSA. Over the six largest cases, the mean wake loss of PSO minus that of PSO-VNS ranges from \NBigGainMin{} to \NBigGainMax{} percentage points, with PSO-VNS lower in \NBigGainPos{} of them.

PSO-VNS ends with a feasible layout in all runs; the other methods do so in \NFeasPSO{} (PSO), \NFeasSSAVNS{} (SSA-VNS), \NFeasSSA{} (SSA), \NFeasLXSSA{} (LX-SSA), \NFeasDE{} (DE), \NFeasVNS{} (VNS) and \NFeasSLSQP\% (MS-SLSQP) of the runs. The feasibility counts of PSO, SSA, LX-SSA and DE are the same for both wind data sets, because as long as every candidate is infeasible, the penalty dominates $F_p$. In the densest cases (500~m, $N=10$), the global best of the PSO phase is feasible at the switch in only \NDenseSwitchFeas\% of the runs, but all runs are feasible after the VNS phase.

Fig.~\ref{fig:conv-max} shows the effect of the second phase directly: PSO-VNS and PSO both keep improving after the switch. Over all feasible runs, the VNS phase removes on average \NPhTwoMean\% (median \NPhTwoMedian\%) of the wake loss left at the end of the PSO phase and turns \NPhTwoMadeFeas{} runs that were still infeasible at the switch into feasible ones, whereas continuing PSO for the same 3,000 calls removes \NPsoContMean\% (median \NPsoContMedian\%). On average the two uses of the second half therefore differ little, in line with Table~\ref{tab:wtl}; the advantage of VNS appears in the larger layouts. The VNS phases of SSA-VNS and RS-VNS remove larger shares (\NPhTwoMeanSSAVNS\% and \NPhTwoMeanRSVNS\%) but start from worse or more often infeasible layouts: at the switch, the best layout is feasible in \NSwitchFeasPSOVNS\% of the PSO-VNS runs, \NSwitchFeasSSAVNS\% of the SSA-VNS runs and \NSwitchFeasRSVNS\% of the RS-VNS runs. VNS alone ranks below all hybrids (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Effect of the PSO Coefficients}
\label{sec:psosetting}
The earlier version of this study ran PSO with $w=0.7$ and $c_1=c_2=2$, which violates \eqref{eq:poli}, on the same 68 cases with the same budget and seeds. With this setting in place of the constriction PSO, PSO had an average rank of \NOldPSORank{} (\NOldPSOPos{} of the eight methods), returned a feasible layout in \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs (fewer than 15 feasible runs in \NOldPSOBelowHalf{} cases), and was significantly worse than the constriction PSO in \NOldPSOWorse{} of the 68 cases and never better (Wilcoxon, 30 seed-paired runs), with a mean wake loss \NOldPSODL{} percentage points higher over the \NOldPSOCases{} cases in which both have at least 15 feasible runs. The constriction setting thus moves PSO from the \NOldPSOPos{} to the \NPSOPos{} rank, ahead of SSA, LX-SSA and SSA-VNS. We draw two conclusions. First, a PSO baseline outside the convergence region understates what PSO achieves on the WFLOP, and comparisons that rely on it can overstate the advantage of a new method; we recommend checking \eqref{eq:poli}, or using the constriction coefficients \eqref{eq:constriction}, whenever PSO serves as a baseline. Second, the salp swarm methods, including our own LX-SSA, do not outperform a correctly configured PSO on this benchmark, which is why PSO rather than SSA forms the first phase of the proposed method.
