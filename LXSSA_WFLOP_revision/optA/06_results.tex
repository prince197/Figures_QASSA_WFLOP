\section{Results on the Benchmark}
\label{sec:results}
This section reports the 68 benchmark cases (\NRunsBenchmark{} runs): the effect of the PSO baseline setting, the overall comparison, and the quality, feasibility and convergence of the solutions; the component analysis follows in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. Per-case means and standard deviations are given in Tables~\ref{S-tab:res-1-500}--\ref{S-tab:res-2-1000} of the supplementary material.

\subsection{Effect of the Baseline Setting}
\label{sec:psosetting}
\label{sec:baseline}
\IfFileExists{analysis/mpce_tab_baseline.tex}{\input{analysis/mpce_tab_baseline.tex}}{}
The setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$, common in PSO baselines for the WFLOP and used in the earlier version of this study, violates the convergence condition~\eqref{eq:poli}. We ran PSO with this setting on the same cases with the same budget and seeds. Compared with the constriction setting~\eqref{eq:constriction}, it returns a feasible layout in only \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs (fewer than 15 feasible runs in \NOldPSOBelowHalf{} cases), is significantly worse in \NOldPSOWorse{} of the 68 cases and never better (Wilcoxon, 30 seed-paired runs), and has a mean wake loss \NOldPSODL{} percentage points higher over the \NOldPSOCases{} cases in which both settings have at least 15 feasible runs. Among the eight methods of Table~\ref{tab:friedman68}, it would place PSO \NOldPSOPos{} (average rank \NOldPSORank) instead of \NPSOPos, where PSO ranks ahead of SSA, LX-SSA and SSA-VNS.
% CHECK-FINAL [C12]: pso_setting: constriction_vs_old L == 0, W >= 34; positions old > constriction == 2; PSOC ahead of SSA, LXSSA, SSABV

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} shows what this does to the conclusions of a comparison. It ranks the method pool of the earlier study (without PSO-VNS) once with each PSO setting, with the same runs of all other methods. With the old setting, \NBaseOldBest{} has the best average rank and PSO ranks behind a salp-swarm hybrid (average ranks: LX-SSA-VNS \NBaseOldLXBVRank, SSA-VNS \NBaseOldSSAVNSRank, PSO \NBaseOldPSORank; W/T/L of the two hybrids against PSO \NBaseOldLXBVvsPSO{} and \NBaseOldSSAVNSvsPSO). With the constriction setting, PSO (\NBaseNewPSORank) ranks ahead of every salp-swarm method, including both hybrids (\NBaseNewLXBVRank{} and \NBaseNewSSAVNSRank; W/T/L \NBaseNewLXBVvsPSO{} and \NBaseNewSSAVNSvsPSO). Changing the coefficients of one baseline thus reverses the order of PSO and the salp-swarm hybrids.
% CHECK-FINAL [C27]: baseline: under the old PSO setting at least one salp-swarm hybrid (SSA-VNS or LX-SSA-VNS) ranks ahead of PSO; under the corrected setting PSO ranks ahead of every salp-swarm method (SSA, LX-SSA, SSA-VNS, LX-SSA-VNS)

This is a property of the setting, not of any particular study: a PSO baseline outside the convergence region understates what PSO achieves on the WFLOP, so a method compared with it can appear stronger than it is. PSO baselines should therefore be checked against~\eqref{eq:poli} or use the coefficients~\eqref{eq:constriction}. On this benchmark, the salp-swarm methods, including our own LX-SSA, do not outperform a correctly configured PSO, which is why PSO forms the first phase of PSO-VNS; all other results use the constriction setting.

\subsection{Overall Comparison}
\label{sec:overall}

Table~\ref{tab:friedman68} summarizes the comparison of the eight methods. The Friedman test \NFriedVerb{} the hypothesis that they perform equally ($\chi^2_F=\NFriedChi$, $p=\NFriedP$). PSO-VNS has the best average rank (\NRankPSOVNS) and alone ranks first in \NSoleBestPSOVNS{} cases, followed by \NRankOrderOthers. In the Holm-adjusted post hoc tests, PSO-VNS ranks significantly better than \NPostHocSigList{} ($p_{\rm Holm}\le\NPostHocSigMaxP$), but not than \NPostHocNonSigList{} ($p_{\rm Holm}=\NPostHocNonSigP$).

The run-level tests in Table~\ref{tab:wtl} locate these differences. Against PSO, which forms its first phase, PSO-VNS is significantly better in \NWtlPSOW{} cases, worse in \NWtlPSOL{} and tied in \NWtlPSOT; its mean wake loss is \NLossPSOVNS\% against \NLossPSO\%, and the difference of the per-case means is not significant (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $p=\NPWrawPSO$). The difference concentrates in the larger cases: for $N\ge10$, PSO-VNS wins \NWtlPSOLargeW{} of the \NNLarge{} cases and loses \NWtlPSOLargeL, with a mean gain of \NGainPSOLarge{} percentage points, whereas for $N<10$ both reach nearly the same wake loss (mean absolute difference \NAbsDiffPSOSmall{} percentage points). Against SSA-VNS, the same design with a salp-swarm first phase, PSO-VNS is significantly better in \NWtlSSAVNSW{} cases and never worse (\NLossPSOVNS\% vs.\ \NLossSSAVNS\%, $p=\NPWrawSSAVNS$). Against SSA, LX-SSA and DE, it is significantly better in \NWtlSSAW, \NWtlLXSSAW{} and \NWtlDEW{} cases, and against VNS and MS-SLSQP in \NWtlVNSW{} and \NWtlSLSQPW{} cases; \NLossesPhrase.

These results support PSO-VNS as a simple reference method, within limits. Under this protocol it is the most consistent of the eight methods: feasible in every run, at least as good as a correctly configured PSO and better for $N\ge10$; with up to 20 times the budget, \NBudgetPhrase{} (Section~\ref{sec:budget}). Its gain over PSO, however, is small and not significant over all cases, and the order of the leading methods depends on the test conditions: with feasible initial layouts, \NFbFeasBestRank{} has the best average rank (Section~\ref{sec:feasinit}); under the Gaussian re-evaluation, \NBestGauss{} ranks first (Section~\ref{sec:gaussian}); and on IEA37 Case Study~1, the best layouts remain below the best published ones (Section~\ref{sec:iea37}).

The wake loss grows with $N$, falls as the farm becomes larger, and is higher for the broader wind rose of Data Set~II (Fig.~\ref{fig:wakeloss}). For $N\le3$, all methods stay within \NLossMaxSmallN\% of the wake-free objective; for 15 turbines in the 1000-m farm of Data Set~I, the mean wake loss is \NLossBigIPSOVNS\% for PSO-VNS, \NLossBigIPSO\% for PSO, \NLossBigISSAVNS\% for SSA-VNS and \NLossBigISSA\% for SSA. Over the six largest cases, the mean wake loss of PSO minus that of PSO-VNS ranges from \NBigGainMin{} to \NBigGainMax{} percentage points, with PSO-VNS lower in \NBigGainPos{} of them.

PSO-VNS ends with a feasible layout in all runs; the other methods do so in \NFeasPSO{} (PSO), \NFeasSSAVNS{} (SSA-VNS), \NFeasSSA{} (SSA), \NFeasLXSSA{} (LX-SSA), \NFeasDE{} (DE), \NFeasVNS{} (VNS) and \NFeasSLSQP\% (MS-SLSQP) of the runs. The counts of PSO, SSA, LX-SSA and DE are the same for both wind data sets, because as long as every candidate is infeasible, the penalty dominates $F_p$. In the densest cases (500~m, $N=10$), the global best of the PSO phase is feasible at the switch in only \NDenseSwitchFeas\% of the runs, but all runs are feasible after the VNS phase.

Fig.~\ref{fig:conv-max} shows the second phase directly: PSO-VNS and PSO both keep improving after the switch. Over all feasible runs, the VNS phase removes on average \NPhTwoMean\% (median \NPhTwoMedian\%) of the wake loss left at the end of the PSO phase and makes \NPhTwoMadeFeas{} runs that were infeasible at the switch feasible, whereas continuing PSO for the same 3,000 calls removes \NPsoContMean\% (median \NPsoContMedian\%). On average the two uses of the second half thus differ little, in line with Table~\ref{tab:wtl}; the advantage of VNS appears in the larger layouts. The VNS phases of SSA-VNS and RS-VNS remove larger shares (\NPhTwoMeanSSAVNS\% and \NPhTwoMeanRSVNS\%) but start from worse or more often infeasible layouts (feasible at the switch in \NSwitchFeasPSOVNS\%, \NSwitchFeasSSAVNS\% and \NSwitchFeasRSVNS\% of the PSO-VNS, SSA-VNS and RS-VNS runs).
