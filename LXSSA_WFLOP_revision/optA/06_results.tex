\label{sec:results}
This section reports the 68 benchmark cases (\NRunsBenchmark{} runs, 6,030 evaluations each); per-case means and standard deviations are given in Tables~\ref{S-tab:res-1-500}--\ref{S-tab:res-2-1000} of the supplementary material.

\subsection{Effect of the Baseline Setting}
\label{sec:psosetting}
\label{sec:baseline}
\IfFileExists{analysis/mpce_tab_baseline.tex}{\input{analysis/mpce_tab_baseline.tex}}{}
The old setting violates the order-2 (variance) stability condition~\eqref{eq:poli}. We ran PSO with it on the same cases with the same budget and seeds. Compared with the constriction setting~\eqref{eq:constriction}, it returns a feasible layout in only \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs (fewer than 15 feasible runs in \NOldPSOBelowHalf{} cases), is significantly worse in \NOldPSOWorse{} of the 68 cases and never better (Wilcoxon, 30 seed-paired runs), and has a mean wake loss \NOldPSODL{} percentage points higher over the \NOldPSOCases{} cases in which both settings have at least 15 feasible runs. As~\eqref{eq:poli} assumes stagnation and unbounded search, the low feasibility plausibly comes from the interplay of the old setting with the clipping to the square, after which velocities are kept (Section~\ref{sec:pso}). In the pool of Table~\ref{tab:friedman68}, the old setting would place PSO \NOldPSOPos{} (average rank \NOldPSORank) instead of \NPSOPos; with the constriction setting, PSO ranks ahead of SSA, LX-SSA and SSA-VNS.
% CHECK-FINAL [C12]: pso_setting: constriction_vs_old L == 0, W >= 34; positions old > constriction == 2; PSOC ahead of SSA, LXSSA, SSABV

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} shows the effect on a comparison: it ranks the methods of Table~\ref{tab:friedman68}, with LX-SSA-VNS in place of PSO-VNS, once with each PSO setting; all other methods use identical runs in both columns, so their rank changes come only from PSO. With the old setting, \NBaseOldBest{} has the best average rank, PSO ranks behind a salp-swarm hybrid, and the W/T/L of LX-SSA-VNS and SSA-VNS against PSO are \NBaseOldLXBVvsPSO{} and \NBaseOldSSAVNSvsPSO. With the constriction setting, PSO ranks ahead of every salp-swarm method, and these W/T/L become \NBaseNewLXBVvsPSO{} and \NBaseNewSSAVNSvsPSO. Changing the coefficients of one baseline thus changes the ranking: at 6,030 evaluations, PSO moves from behind to ahead of the salp-swarm hybrids. The constriction coefficients are standard values~\cite{Clerc2002}, not tuned on these cases; the other methods keep standard, untuned settings. The old setting was run only at 6,030 evaluations, and with more evaluations the order changes again: on the six largest cases at 120,030 evaluations, SSA-VNS ranks ahead of PSO (average ranks \NBudRankSSAVNSOneTwentyK{} and \NBudRankPSOOneTwentyK; Section~\ref{sec:budget}).
% CHECK-FINAL [C27]: baseline: under the old PSO setting at least one salp-swarm hybrid (SSA-VNS or LX-SSA-VNS) ranks ahead of PSO; under the corrected setting PSO ranks ahead of every salp-swarm method (SSA, LX-SSA, SSA-VNS, LX-SSA-VNS)
% 120,030 statement: feasbudget ranks, six largest cases: SSABV (4.17) < PSOC (5.83); no CHECK id yet (propose: rank SSABV[120030] < rank PSOC[120030])
This is a property of the setting, not of any particular study: on this benchmark, at 6,030 evaluations and with clipping, a PSO baseline that violates~\eqref{eq:poli} performs markedly worse, so a method compared with it can appear stronger than it is. Under these conditions the salp-swarm methods, including our own LX-SSA, do not outperform a correctly configured PSO, which is why PSO forms the first phase of PSO-VNS.

In an exploratory, post hoc split, the advantage of PSO-VNS is concentrated in Data Set~II with $N\ge10$, where it has the lower mean wake loss in \NCmPSOVNSvsPSOdsIILargeWins{} of \NCmPSOVNSvsPSOdsIILargeN{} cases (unadjusted Wilcoxon $p=\NCmPSOVNSvsPSOdsIILargeP$; significant run-level wins/losses \NWtlPSOdsIILargeW/\NWtlPSOdsIILargeL).
