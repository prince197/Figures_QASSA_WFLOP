\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}
The three findings share one implication: in continuous WFLOP, which method appears best depends as much on the comparison protocol as on the methods. The setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$ is a property of the PSO parameters, not of any particular study: it violates \eqref{eq:poli}, and with it PSO ranks \NOldPSOPos{} of the eight methods of the main comparison (feasible in \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs), while \NBaseOldBest{} ranks first in the method pool of the previous study; with the constriction coefficients, PSO ranks \NPSOPos{} in the main comparison, \NBaseNewBest{} ranks first in that pool, and PSO is ahead of every salp-swarm method in both (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}).
% CHECK-FINAL [C12]: old PSO setting understates PSO; constriction PSO second, ahead of SSA, LX-SSA, SSA-VNS
% CHECK-FINAL [C27]: old setting: at least one salp-swarm hybrid ahead of PSO; corrected: PSO ahead of every salp-swarm method
An advantage over PSO is therefore informative only if the PSO baseline is convergent, and a swarm phase in a hybrid earns its place only if it beats a random-sampling control with the same budget, which the PSO phase does and the salp phases do not. PSO-VNS is a reasonable reference because it combines two standard components, adds one parameter ($\omega$) and is feasible in every run; its gain over PSO, however, is not significant over all cases ($p=\NPWPSO$) and appears mainly for $N\ge10$.
% CHECK-FINAL [C15]: SSA-VNS tied with RS-VNS; [C14]: PSO start beats RS-VNS
% CHECK-FINAL [C04]: case-mean p >= 0.05; [C05]: W > L for N >= 10; [C08]: feasible in every run

For practitioners, this means that with random starts and a penalized objective, PSO-VNS (preferably with $\omega=0.75$, Section~\ref{sec:split}) or a constriction PSO is a sound first choice, whereas VNS alone is competitive when a feasible start is available. Since the energy values are benchmark values, a layout should be re-evaluated, and preferably re-optimized, with the wake and power models of the site before it informs a decision. For researchers who propose WFLOP metaheuristics, we recommend the following, all of which are applied in this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item configure every baseline inside its convergence region (for PSO, check \eqref{eq:poli} or use \eqref{eq:constriction}) and report all settings;
\item give all methods the same budget of objective calls, counting the initial population and every gradient or finite-difference call;
\item use seed-paired runs that start from the same initial population;
\item rank feasibility-aware: report the share of feasible runs and compare wake losses on feasible layouts only;
\item test both on the per-case means and on the runs of each case, with a multiplicity correction such as Holm's, and report non-significant results;
\item in ablations of hybrids, include a random-sampling control and the local method alone;
\item report feasible-start and model-robustness checks, and publish code and data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The benchmark uses the top-hat Jensen wake, the linear Kusiak--Song power curve, circular farms, a $4D$ spacing and a prescribed $N$; its energies are benchmark values, and when the final layouts are re-evaluated with a Gaussian wake, \NBestGauss{} ranks ahead of PSO-VNS. We did not re-optimize with the alternative models.
% CHECK-FINAL [C22]: Gaussian: PSOC first, PSOBV second
The budget study reaches 120,030 evaluations but covers only the six largest cases and the Horns Rev~1 block, and the largest layouts have 36 turbines. The PSO and VNS settings are untuned defaults, and the pre-specified $\omega=0.5$ is not the best split.
% CHECK-FINAL [C18]: split favors 75%
With feasibility-preserving initialization, \NFbFeasBestRank{} ranks first, and PSO and DE do not move from the best initial layout, because velocity and difference moves between feasible layouts with arbitrary turbine labels make turbines overlap and are rejected by the feasibility-first comparison; our statements on PSO-VNS therefore refer to random starts. The only real site is a 16-turbine block of Horns Rev~1, and on IEA37 Case Study~1 the best PSO-VNS layouts still differ by \NIEAGapSixteenThirtyK\% and \NIEAGapThirtySixThirtyK\% from the best feasible published layouts (reported for a gradient-based method, \TBD{verify against Baker et al.\ (2019)}). Our statement on the setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$ rests on the convergence theory and our benchmark; we did not re-run the comparisons of other studies. Finally, terrain, mixed turbine types, cables, grid connection, noise, environmental constraints and economic objectives such as the levelized cost of energy are outside our scope.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper revisited metaheuristic comparisons for continuous WFLOP under a controlled protocol on 68 benchmark cases, a Horns Rev~1 block and IEA37 Case Study~1. First, the baseline configuration changes the conclusions: the PSO setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$ violates the convergence condition and places PSO behind salp-swarm hybrids, whereas the constriction setting places it ahead of every salp-swarm method. Second, in two-phase hybrids, the VNS phase improves every swarm, but the swarm phase helps only if the swarm is effective: the SSA and LX-SSA phases are statistically tied with random sampling, the Laplace step gives no gain, and a PSO phase beats random sampling, VNS alone and the salp phases. Third, PSO-VNS is a simple and consistent reference method: it is feasible in every run, has the best average rank (\NRankPSOVNS) and the best rank at all three budgets, is better than PSO for $N\ge10$, and its best Horns Rev~1 layout exceeds the installed one; yet it is not significantly better than PSO over all cases, \NFbFeasBestRank{} alone is best from feasible starts, \NBestGauss{} ranks first under the Gaussian re-evaluation, and on IEA37 it stays below the best published layouts.
