\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}
In the WFLOP, which method appears best can depend on the protocol as strongly as on the methods. \emph{Baseline configuration:} replacing the old setting, which violates the order-2 stability condition~\eqref{eq:poli}, by the constriction setting moves PSO from position \NBaseOldPSOPos{} to \NBaseNewPSOPos{} in Table~\ref{tab:baseline}, ahead of every salp-swarm method at 6,030 evaluations. Instrumented runs show why: the old swarm stays \NDSpreadRatio{} times wider, and only \NDFeasPartOld\% of its particle evaluations are feasible (constriction: \NDFeasPartNew\%). An advantage over PSO is informative only if the PSO baseline is convergent.

\emph{Component analysis:} a swarm phase must also beat random sampling of equal cost, and the control must be strong. Sampling in the bounding square (RS-VNS) is weak (\NDRsRunsNoFeasSample{} of its runs never sample a feasible layout; in the disc: \NRSDRunsNoFeasSample). Against sampling in the disc (RSD-VNS), the SSA phase gives no gain (\NCmSSAVNSvsRSDVNSDL~pp) and the LX-SSA phase is worse (\NCmLXSSAVNSvsRSDVNSDL~pp), so the small SSA gain over RS-VNS only reflects that control's weakness; the PSO phase beats both clearly (\NCmPSOVNSvsRSDVNSDL~pp). At 6,030 evaluations, the second phase is essentially one compass-search descent (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so this analysis measures the value of a start for a local search, not of the VNS neighborhoods.

\emph{PSO and PSO-VNS:} a well-configured PSO and PSO-VNS are practically equivalent on the case means (90\% interval \NEqPSOVNSvsPSOCI~pp; mean gain \NXEnPSOVNSvsPSOPct\% of benchmark AEP), with no significant difference in spread; the rank flip under the Gaussian model is consistent with this. For benchmark-type problems with random starts, a constriction PSO is a sound first choice and PSO-VNS a more reliable one; from feasible starts, VNS alone is competitive. For WFLOP comparisons, we recommend~\cite{BartzBeielstein2020,LaTorre2021}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item use baseline settings with documented convergence or standard defaults (for PSO, \eqref{eq:poli} or \eqref{eq:constriction}), report them, and verify their behavior with instrumented runs;
\item give all methods the same budget, counting the initial population and gradient calls, and pair runs by seed;
\item use feasibility-aware ranking and report feasible runs;
\item test case means and runs with a multiplicity correction~\cite{Derrac2011}, and report threshold and, for nested cases, cluster-level robustness;
\item test practical equivalence with a stated margin~\cite{Lakens2017,Benavoli2017}; non-significance is not equivalence;
\item in ablations of hybrids, include the local method alone and an in-site random-sampling control of equal cost;
\item report feasible-start and model-robustness checks; publish code and data.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The benchmark uses the top-hat Jensen wake, the linear Kusiak--Song power curve, coarse direction bins that optimized layouts can exploit, circular farms, a $4D$ spacing and a prescribed $N$. The 68 cases form \NXClusters{} clusters with nested $N$, so the p-values describe this benchmark. The $N\ge10$ split is post hoc, and the equivalence margin was set after the primary analysis, so we report the smallest margin at which equivalence holds (\NEqPSOVNSvsPSOMin~pp for PSO-VNS vs.\ PSO). At 6,030 evaluations, the VNS neighborhoods are hardly exercised; the budget study covers only six cases and Horns Rev~1, and the old PSO setting was run only at 6,030 evaluations. DE, VNS and $\omega=0.5$ are untuned defaults.
% CHECK-EXTRA [X10]: six clusters (cluster exceptions [X11]/[X14]/[X33] reported in 05/07); CHECK-FINAL [C49]: minimal margin NEqPSOVNSvsPSOMin < NEqMargin; CHECK-DIAG [D08]-[D10]
The pool lacks CMA-ES~\cite{HansenOstermeier2001} or L-SHADE, and MS-SLSQP uses finite-difference gradients on a discontinuous objective. A feasible random search~\cite{Feng2015} could be a still stronger control than sampling in the disc. From feasible starts, \NFbFeasBestRank{} ranks first and PSO and DE stall (Section~\ref{sec:feasinit}; mechanism observed, not derived); our statements on PSO-VNS refer to random starts.
% CHECK-FINAL [C28]: feasible starts: VNS alone first; CHECK-DIAG [D13]/[D14]: no improvement, no pbest update
The Horns Rev~1 comparison tests the optimizers, not the installed design (Section~\ref{sec:hornsrev-site}); on IEA37, the best PSO-VNS layouts at 30,030 evaluations \NIEAPhrase. Turbulence and fatigue loads (critical at $4D$), wake steering, blockage, terrain, cables, electrical losses and cost objectives are outside our scope; all AEP values are gross.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Three findings emerge. First, the baseline configuration changes the conclusions: the old PSO setting, which violates the order-2 stability condition and rarely samples feasible layouts, puts PSO behind salp-swarm hybrids, whereas the constriction setting puts it ahead of every salp-swarm method. Second, a PSO phase gives the second phase (here one compass-search descent) a clearly better start than random sampling, whereas salp-swarm phases add nothing beyond random sampling. Third, a well-configured PSO and PSO-VNS are practically equivalent on average ($\pm\NEqMargin$~pp); PSO-VNS adds feasibility reliability and gains in the larger Data Set~II layouts. It is thus a consistent reference method, not a superior optimizer. Future work will address larger budgets and farms, stronger controls, better wake models and economic objectives.
