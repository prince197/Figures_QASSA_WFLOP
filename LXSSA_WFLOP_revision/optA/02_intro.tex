\section{Introduction}

The positions of the turbines in a wind farm are fixed at the planning stage and determine, for the lifetime of the farm, how much of the available wind energy it converts. An upstream turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the gross energy yield, and it also shapes the collector system and grid connection~\cite{Srikakulapu2018,Hou2019}. As the wake loss is a share of the wake-free energy, a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point between two optimizers is a lifetime energy difference of that size. The wind farm layout optimization problem (WFLOP) places a prescribed number of turbines in a site to minimize the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints; we address only this wake-loss part of layout design, for farms of up to 36 turbines. Its nonlinear objective, the coupling of all turbines by the wakes and the nonconvex spacing constraints have made the WFLOP a popular test bed for metaheuristics.

Early studies placed turbines on a grid with genetic algorithms (GAs)~\cite{Mosetti1994,Grady2005}. The continuous, wind-distribution-based model of Kusiak and Song~\cite{Kusiak2010}, which we use because it is a common reference for metaheuristics, has been solved with ant colony optimization and particle filtering~\cite{Eroglu2012,Eroglu2013} and biogeography-based optimization~\cite{Bansal2017}; GA, particle swarm optimization (PSO), differential evolution (DE) and simulated annealing were compared in~\cite{Elkinton2008}, and PSO has been used to reduce wake losses~\cite{Asaah2021}. Later work used informed GAs~\cite{Ju2019,Bai2022}, deterministic refinement~\cite{Nagpal2021}, variable neighborhood search (VNS) for large offshore farms~\cite{Cazzaro2022}, pseudo-gradients~\cite{Quaeghebeur2021}, Gaussian wake models~\cite{Tao2020} and surrogates~\cite{Yang2023,Wang2024,Li2025}.

Guidelines for comparing metaheuristics are well established: controlled rather than competitive experiments~\cite{Hooker1995}, equal budgets, reported parameter settings, component analysis of the proposed method and paired nonparametric tests with multiplicity correction~\cite{Demsar2006,Derrac2011,BartzBeielstein2020,LaTorre2021}, sample sizes chosen for a stated power~\cite{Campelo2019}, and equivalence or Bayesian analyses when the question is whether two methods differ by a practically relevant amount~\cite{Benavoli2017,Lakens2017}. Critical studies have shown that several metaphor-based algorithms, the salp swarm algorithm (SSA)~\cite{Mirjalili2017} among them, reuse known search operators~\cite{Sorensen2015,CamachoVillalon2023,Castelli2022}. In the WFLOP, comparisons with a common objective exist, namely the GECCO layout competition~\cite{Wilson2018} and the IEA Wind Task~37 studies~\cite{Baker2019,Thomas2023}, in which optimizers applied by experienced users reached different layouts of similar energy yield; reviews of the classical benchmark wind scenarios list many metaheuristics compared under differing budgets and settings~\cite{Azlan2021}, and random search is itself a WFLOP method~\cite{Feng2015}. These studies compare methods as submitted or as published; they do not examine how the configuration of a baseline or the choice of control changes which method appears best. Four issues matter here. First, a baseline may be configured outside its working range: the expected positions of PSO particles converge (order-1 stability) for a wide range of the inertia weight $w$ and the acceleration coefficients $c_1$, $c_2$, but their variance converges (order-2 stability) only in a smaller region~\cite{Poli2009,Bonyadi2017,Cleghorn2018}, and the setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$, which keeps the acceleration coefficients of the original PSO~\cite{Kennedy1995}, lies outside it. Second, budgets counted in iterations, or without the gradient evaluations of gradient-based solvers, are not equal. Third, penalty-based metaheuristics may return infeasible layouts, which must be ranked consistently. Fourth, conclusions drawn from mean values without paired tests and multiplicity correction~\cite{Benavoli2016} may not hold, and a nonsignificant difference is not evidence of equivalence~\cite{Lakens2017}. For two-phase hybrids, a further question is whether the first phase contributes more than a random start would.

Our thesis is that the conclusions of such comparisons depend strongly on how the baselines are configured and how the comparison is controlled. Under a controlled protocol, a well-configured PSO and a simple two-phase design, PSO followed by the basic VNS~\cite{Mladenovic1997,Hansen2001} (PSO-VNS), rank first and \NPSOPos{} of eight methods and are practically equivalent on average, while salp-swarm phases add little beyond random sampling and the Laplace step makes the hybrid worse. The salp-swarm methods are SSA and the Laplacian SSA (LX-SSA)~\cite{Solanki2023}, proposed earlier by two of the authors; PSO-VNS is a sequential hybrid in the memetic sense, not a new PSO variant.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The application of established benchmarking practice~\cite{Derrac2011,BartzBeielstein2020,LaTorre2021} to the continuous WFLOP (equal budgets that count every objective and gradient evaluation, seed-paired runs, case-mean and run-level tests with Holm correction, re-evaluation under alternative modeling assumptions, public code and data), with three elements that, to our knowledge, have not been reported for this problem: feasibility-aware ranking, a random-sampling control of equal cost for two-phase hybrids, and an equivalence analysis with a margin of $\pm\NEqMargin$ percentage points (pp) of wake loss, fixed after the primary analysis and reported with the minimal equivalence margin (two one-sided tests and a Bayesian signed-rank test~\cite{Lakens2017,Benavoli2017}). The main conclusions do not change with the qualification threshold for feasible runs, with farm clusters instead of cases as the units, or under one Holm correction over all main-text tests. We apply the protocol to eight methods on 68 benchmark cases, a 16-turbine block of the Horns Rev~1 offshore farm and IEA Wind Task~37 (IEA37) Case Study~1 (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}, \ref{sec:beyond} and~\ref{sec:robustness}).
