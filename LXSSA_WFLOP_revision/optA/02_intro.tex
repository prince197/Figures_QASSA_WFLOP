\section{Introduction}

The positions of the turbines in a wind farm are fixed at the planning stage and determine, for the lifetime of the farm, how much of the available wind energy it delivers to the grid. An upstream turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the energy yield on which capacity planning and the design of the collector and grid connection rely~\cite{Srikakulapu2018,Hou2019}. The wind farm layout optimization problem (WFLOP) places a prescribed number of turbines in a site to minimize the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints. The objective is nonlinear, the wakes couple the positions of all turbines, and the feasible region is bounded by many nonconvex spacing constraints, which has made WFLOP a popular test bed for metaheuristics.

Early studies placed turbines on a grid with genetic algorithms (GAs)~\cite{Mosetti1994,Grady2005}. The continuous, wind-distribution-based model of Kusiak and Song~\cite{Kusiak2010}, which we also use, has been solved with ant colony optimization and particle filtering~\cite{Eroglu2012,Eroglu2013} and biogeography-based optimization~\cite{Bansal2017}; GA, particle swarm optimization (PSO), differential evolution (DE) and simulated annealing were compared in~\cite{Elkinton2008}, and PSO has been used to reduce wake losses~\cite{Asaah2021}. Later work used informed GAs~\cite{Ju2019,Bai2022}, deterministic refinement~\cite{Nagpal2021}, variable neighborhood search (VNS) for large offshore farms~\cite{Cazzaro2022}, pseudo-gradients~\cite{Quaeghebeur2021}, Gaussian wake models~\cite{Tao2020} and surrogates~\cite{Yang2023,Wang2024,Li2025}. The IEA Wind Task~37 case study~\cite{Baker2019} showed that, even with a common wake model, different optimizers reach clearly different layouts.

Many of these studies propose a new or modified metaheuristic and show that it outperforms a set of reference methods. The benchmarking literature has long warned that such comparisons are hard to interpret~\cite{Sorensen2015,BartzBeielstein2020}, and four issues matter for the WFLOP. First, the baselines may be configured outside their working range: the particles of PSO converge only if the inertia weight $w$ and the acceleration coefficients $c_1$, $c_2$ lie in a known region~\cite{Clerc2002,Poli2009}, and the setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$, common in PSO baselines and used in the earlier version of this study, lies outside it. Second, budgets counted in iterations, or without the gradient calls of gradient-based solvers, are not equal. Third, the metaheuristics handle the constraints by penalties and may return infeasible layouts, which must be ranked consistently. Fourth, conclusions drawn from mean values without paired tests and multiplicity correction~\cite{Demsar2006,Benavoli2016} may not hold. For two-phase hybrids, a further question is whether the first phase contributes more than a random start would.

This paper revisits the comparison of metaheuristics for the continuous WFLOP under a protocol that controls these factors. Our thesis is that the conclusions of such comparisons depend strongly on how the baselines are configured and how the comparison is controlled, and that under a controlled protocol a simple two-phase design, a convergent PSO followed by the basic VNS~\cite{Mladenovic1997,Hansen2001} (PSO-VNS), is the most consistent performer, whereas the phases of the salp swarm algorithm (SSA)~\cite{Mirjalili2017} and the Laplace step of the Laplacian SSA (LX-SSA)~\cite{Solanki2023}, proposed earlier by two of the authors, add nothing beyond random sampling. With the old PSO setting, PSO ranks \NOldPSOPos{} of eight methods and is feasible in \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs, and salp-swarm hybrids appear stronger; with the constriction coefficients of Clerc and Kennedy~\cite{Clerc2002}, PSO ranks \NPSOPos{} and ahead of every salp-swarm method, including an SSA-VNS hybrid that we had developed first.

We report this as a property of the setting, not as a finding about any particular study. We keep the Jensen wake model, Weibull wind data and the Kusiak--Song power model for comparability with the literature and examine these choices separately. Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled evaluation protocol for the continuous WFLOP: equal budgets that count every objective and gradient call, seed-paired runs, feasibility-aware ranking, case-mean and run-level tests with Holm correction, an ablation with a random-sampling control, re-evaluation under alternative modeling assumptions, and public code and data. We apply it to eight methods on 68 benchmark cases, a 16-turbine block of the Horns Rev~1 offshore farm and IEA Wind Task~37 Case Study~1 (IEA37) (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}, \ref{sec:beyond} and~\ref{sec:robustness}).
\item Evidence that the PSO baseline setting reverses the ranking of the methods: the setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$ violates the convergence condition of PSO, and replacing it by the constriction setting moves PSO from behind salp-swarm hybrids to ahead of every salp-swarm method (Sections~\ref{sec:pso} and~\ref{sec:psosetting}, Table~\ref{tab:baseline}).

\item A component analysis of two-phase swarm--VNS hybrids: the VNS phase helps every swarm, but the swarm phase helps only if the swarm is effective. The SSA and LX-SSA phases are statistically tied with random sampling (RS-VNS) and the Laplace step gives no gain, whereas a PSO phase beats random sampling, VNS alone and the salp-swarm phases (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\item PSO-VNS as a simple reference method, with an account of where it wins and where it does not. It obtains the best average rank (\NRankPSOVNS), is feasible in every run, keeps the best rank at all three budgets, and its best layout for the Horns Rev~1 block exceeds the energy production of the installed layout; but it is not significantly better than PSO over all cases (case-mean Wilcoxon $p=\NPWPSO$), VNS alone ranks first with feasible starts, PSO ranks first under a Gaussian wake model, and on IEA37 a gap to the published optima remains (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:robustness}). From these results we derive practical recommendations for benchmarking WFLOP methods.
