\section{Introduction}

The positions of the turbines in a wind farm are fixed at the planning stage and determine, for the lifetime of the farm, how much of the available wind energy it converts. An upstream turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the gross energy yield, and it also shapes the collector system and grid connection~\cite{Srikakulapu2018,Hou2019}. As the wake loss is a share of the wake-free energy, a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point between two optimizers is a lifetime energy difference of that size. The wind farm layout optimization problem (WFLOP) places a prescribed number of turbines in a site to minimize the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints; we address only this wake-loss part of layout design, for farms of up to 36 turbines. Its nonlinear objective, the coupling of all turbines by the wakes and the nonconvex spacing constraints have made the WFLOP a popular test bed for metaheuristics.

Early studies placed turbines on a grid with genetic algorithms (GAs)~\cite{Mosetti1994,Grady2005}. The continuous, wind-distribution-based model of Kusiak and Song~\cite{Kusiak2010}, which we use because it is a common reference for metaheuristics, has been solved with ant colony optimization and particle filtering~\cite{Eroglu2012,Eroglu2013} and biogeography-based optimization~\cite{Bansal2017}; GA, particle swarm optimization (PSO), differential evolution (DE) and simulated annealing were compared in~\cite{Elkinton2008}, and PSO has been used to reduce wake losses~\cite{Asaah2021}. Later work used informed GAs~\cite{Ju2019,Bai2022}, deterministic refinement~\cite{Nagpal2021}, variable neighborhood search (VNS) for large offshore farms~\cite{Cazzaro2022}, pseudo-gradients~\cite{Quaeghebeur2021}, Gaussian wake models~\cite{Tao2020} and surrogates~\cite{Yang2023,Wang2024,Li2025}. The IEA Wind Task~37 case study~\cite{Baker2019} showed that, even with a common wake model, different optimizers reach clearly different layouts.

Many of these studies introduce a new or modified metaheuristic and compare it with reference methods; the benchmarking literature notes that the outcome of such comparisons depends on how they are set up~\cite{Sorensen2015,BartzBeielstein2020}. Four issues matter for the WFLOP. First, a baseline may be configured outside its working range: the expected positions of PSO particles converge (order-1 stability) for a wide range of the inertia weight $w$ and the acceleration coefficients $c_1$, $c_2$, but their variance converges (order-2 stability) only in a smaller region~\cite{Poli2009,Cleghorn2018}, and the setting $w=0.7$, $c_1=c_2=2$, which keeps the acceleration coefficients of the original PSO~\cite{Kennedy1995}, lies outside it. Second, budgets counted in iterations, or without the gradient evaluations of gradient-based solvers, are not equal. Third, penalty-based metaheuristics may return infeasible layouts, which must be ranked consistently. Fourth, conclusions drawn from mean values without paired tests and multiplicity correction~\cite{Demsar2006,Benavoli2016} may not hold. For two-phase hybrids, a further question is whether the first phase contributes more than a random start would.

This paper revisits such comparisons for the continuous WFLOP under a protocol that controls these factors. Our thesis is that their conclusions depend strongly on how the baselines are configured and how the comparison is controlled. Under a controlled protocol, a simple two-phase design, a convergent PSO followed by the basic VNS~\cite{Mladenovic1997,Hansen2001} (PSO-VNS), is the most consistent of the eight methods compared, while salp-swarm phases add little beyond random sampling and the Laplace step makes the hybrid worse. The salp-swarm methods are the salp swarm algorithm (SSA)~\cite{Mirjalili2017,Castelli2022} and the Laplacian SSA (LX-SSA)~\cite{Solanki2023}, proposed earlier by two of the authors; PSO-VNS is a sequential hybrid in the memetic sense, not a new PSO variant.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The application of established benchmarking practice~\cite{Demsar2006,BartzBeielstein2020} to the continuous WFLOP (equal budgets that count every objective evaluation, including gradient evaluations, seed-paired runs, case-mean and run-level tests with Holm correction, re-evaluation under alternative modeling assumptions, public code and data), with two WFLOP-specific elements: feasibility-aware ranking and a random-sampling control for two-phase hybrids. We apply it to eight methods on 68 benchmark cases, a 16-turbine block of the Horns Rev~1 offshore farm and IEA Wind Task~37 (IEA37) Case Study~1 (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}, \ref{sec:beyond} and~\ref{sec:robustness}).
\item Evidence that the PSO baseline setting changes the order of PSO and the salp-swarm methods: this setting violates the order-2 stability condition; with it, PSO is feasible in \NOldPSOFeas\% of the runs and, in a comparison of salp-swarm hybrids with standard baselines, ranks behind both hybrids; with the constriction setting~\cite{Clerc2002} it ranks first there and \NPSOPos{} in the main comparison, ahead of every salp-swarm method, at 6,030 evaluations. At 120,030 evaluations, SSA-VNS ranks ahead of PSO on the six largest cases (average rank \NBudRankSSAVNSOneTwentyK{} vs \NBudRankPSOOneTwentyK; Sections~\ref{sec:pso}, \ref{sec:psosetting} and~\ref{sec:budget}, Table~\ref{tab:baseline}).

\item A component analysis of two-phase swarm--VNS hybrids with a random-sampling control (RS-VNS), a minimum bar, as uniform samples in the bounding square are rarely feasible. \item PSO-VNS as a simple reference method, with an account of where it wins and where it does not. On the six largest cases, it has the best average rank at all three budgets (exploratory, no test; first in \NBudFirstPSOVNSOneTwentyK{} of the six cases at 120,030 evaluations), and on the Horns Rev~1 block it has the highest mean yield at 6,030 and 30,030 evaluations, with \NHRAboveThirtyKPSOVNS{} of 30 runs at 30,030 evaluations above the modeled yield of the installed block (installed spacing $7D$, optimized $4D$). From these results we derive recommendations for benchmarking WFLOP methods (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
