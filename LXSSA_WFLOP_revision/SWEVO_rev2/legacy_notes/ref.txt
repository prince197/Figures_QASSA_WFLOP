Abstract
We compare eight wind farm layout optimizers on the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark
(68 cases, 15◦ rose) with 6,030 evaluations from random starts, feasibility-aware ranking
and equivalence tests. First, the particle swarm optimization (PSO) setting w = 0.7,
c1 = c2 = 2 lies beyond the order-2 (variance) stability boundary; in a sweep of c1 = c2 ,
feasibility starts to degrade there and falls progressively beyond it. With it, PSO ranks
behind both salp-swarm hybrids in the eight-method pool of an earlier comparison; with
the constriction setting, it ranks first there. Second, a salp-swarm (SSA) first phase
adds nothing beyond equal-cost random sampling in the farm (a gain above 0.05 per-
centage points (pp) of wake loss is ruled out across seeds and cases), the Laplacian SSA
as published is worse, and a PSO phase gives a clear gain; at this budget the second
phase, variable neighborhood search (VNS), acts essentially as one compass-search de-
scent. Third, PSO followed by VNS (PSO-VNS) has the best average rank with 15◦ , 5◦
and 1◦ direction bins, also under a Gaussian wake at 1◦ , but its average advantage over a
well-configured PSO is small: within ±0.05 pp across seeds and cases, not farm clusters,
under the 15◦ rose, and a significant −0.043 pp under a 1◦ rose; case-level differences go
both ways. PSO-VNS reaches feasibility more reliably (Horns Rev 1 block: 30/30 vs.
19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 ).
Keywords: Benchmarking, equivalence testing, particle swarm optimization,
random-sampling control, wake effect, wind farm layout optimization

 3   1. Introduction
 4       The positions of the turbines in a wind farm are fixed at the planning stage and
 5   determine, for its lifetime, how much of the available wind energy it converts. An upstream
 6   turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of
 7   the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the gross energy
 8   yield, and shapes the collector system and grid connection [1, 2]. As the wake loss is
 9   a share of the wake-free energy, a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point is,
10   within a given wake model, a lifetime energy difference of that size. The wind farm layout
11   optimization problem (WFLOP) places a given number of turbines in a site to minimize
12   the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints; we address only this
13   wake-loss part of layout design, for farms of up to 36 turbines.
14       For swarm and evolutionary computation, the continuous WFLOP is also a represen-
15   tative constrained benchmark. Its 2N coordinates are continuous, the objective is multi-
16   modal and, with a top-hat wake, discontinuous; the constraints are a site boundary and
17   N (N − 1)/2 pairwise spacing constraints, so the feasible region is nonconvex and becomes
18   a small part of the bounding box as N grows; one evaluation is cheap enough for thou-
19   sands of runs; and standard instances with published results exist, from the Kusiak–Song
20   cases [3] to the IEA Wind Task 37 case studies [4]. These properties, which it shares with
21   many engineering design problems, have made it a common test bed for new metaheuris-
22   tics [5], including swarm methods such as particle swarm optimization (PSO) [6, 7] and,
23   more recently, metaphor-based algorithms such as the salp swarm algorithm (SSA) [8, 9].
24   What such studies can show depends on how the comparison is run.
25       A new algorithm is usually justified by a comparison with baselines, and its claimed
26   advantage is only as reliable as that comparison. Guidelines for experiments with meta-
27   heuristics [10–13] and critiques of metaphor-based algorithms [14, 15] both point to the
28   same weak spots: baselines run with poor or unreported settings, budgets that are not
29   equal, constraint violations that are ignored in the ranking, and statistics that confuse the
30   absence of a significant difference with equivalence. In the WFLOP, the GECCO layout
31   competition [16] and the IEA Wind Task 37 studies [4, 17] compared optimizers on a
32   common objective, and reviews list many metaheuristics tested under differing budgets
33   and settings [5]; these studies compare methods as submitted or as published, not how
34   the configuration of a baseline or the choice of control changes which method appears best

45       Our thesis is that the conclusions of such comparisons depend strongly on how the
46   baselines are configured and how the comparison is controlled. Under a controlled proto-
47   col on the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark at 6,030 evaluations from random starts,
48   a simple two-phase design, PSO followed by the basic variable neighborhood search
49   (VNS) [24, 25] (PSO-VNS), and a well-configured PSO rank first and second of eight
50   methods and differ little on average, whereas salp-swarm first phases add nothing beyond
51   random sampling in the farm and the Laplacian SSA (LX-SSA) [26], proposed earlier by
52   two of the authors, is worse as published. PSO-VNS is a sequential hybrid in the memetic
53   sense [27], not a new PSO variant. Our contributions are:
54     1. Protocol. Established practice [11–13] applied to the continuous WFLOP (equal
55        budgets counting gradient evaluations, seed-paired runs, Holm-corrected tests, pub-
56        lic code and data), with three elements not reported for this problem to our knowl-
57        edge: feasibility-aware ranking, equal-cost random-sampling controls for hybrids,
58        and an equivalence analysis [23, 28] at the seed, case and cluster level with a post
59        hoc margin of ±0.05 percentage points (pp) of wake loss; applied to 68 benchmark
60        cases, a Horns Rev 1 block and IEA37 Case Study 1 (Sections 6 and 9). The proto-
61        col is not specific to the WFLOP and carries over to other constrained continuous
62        problems solved by penalty-based swarm and evolutionary methods.
63     2. Baseline configuration. The PSO setting w = 0.7, c1 = c2 = 2 (run without velocity
64        clamping) lies beyond the order-2 boundary (Proposition 1); in a sweep of c1 = c2 ,
65        feasibility starts to degrade at the boundary and falls progressively beyond it, and
66        the setting reverses the order of PSO and the salp-swarm hybrids (Section 7.1). A
67        stability condition from PSO theory thus serves as a practical check of a baseline
68        before a comparison is run.
69     3. Component analysis. Against random sampling in the farm (RSD-VNS), salp-swarm
70        first phases add nothing and LX-SSA as published (at twice the evaluations per

73        that samples the bounding square instead of the farm is too weak: against it, the
74        SSA phase shows a small gain that disappears against sampling in the farm, so the
75        choice of control itself changes the verdict on a first phase.
76     4. PSO-VNS versus PSO. PSO-VNS has the best average rank with 15◦ , 5◦ and 1◦
77        direction bins, also under a Gaussian wake at 1◦ , but its average advantage over a

80        differences go both ways. We report where it gains (Horns Rev 1 feasibility, larger
81        Data Set II layouts) and where it does not (feasible starts, IEA37), and derive rec-
82        ommendations (Sections 7.2–11). Because the PSO phase of PSO-VNS reproduces

 94   2. Related Work
 95       We review five strands of work and state, for each, what is new here; no new optimizer
 96   is proposed.

 97   2.1. Wind Farm Layout Optimization with Metaheuristics
 98       Discrete and continuous formulations. Early studies placed turbines in the cells of
 99   a square grid and optimized the cell occupation with genetic algorithms (GAs) [29, 30];
100   binary PSO with time-varying acceleration coefficients was applied to the same kind of
101   grid [6]. A grid makes the problem combinatorial but restricts the layouts that can be
102   found. Kusiak and Song [3] formulated a continuous model with turbine coordinates in a
103   circular farm, a discretized wind rose, a Jensen wake [31, 32] and explicit boundary and
104   spacing constraints. This model, with two wind data sets [3, 33] and several farm radii,
105   has become a common reference for metaheuristics and defines the benchmark used here
106   (Section 6). On it, or on close variants, ant colony optimization and particle filtering [34,
107   35], biogeography-based optimization [33] and random search inside the feasible space [36]
108   have been proposed, and GA, PSO, differential evolution (DE) and simulated annealing
109   were compared on offshore layouts [37]; PSO has reduced wake losses in [7].
110       Newer metaheuristics and hybrids. Later work used self-informed GAs [38], an adap-
111   tive evolutionary algorithm combined with Monte Carlo tree search [39], deterministic
112   refinement techniques [40] and VNS for large offshore farms [41]. Metaphor-based algo-
113   rithms have also been applied; for example, the water cycle algorithm was compared with
114   the SSA and the grey wolf optimizer on turbine placement [9], and the Laplacian SSA of
115   two of the authors [26] was combined with VNS in an earlier comparison that motivated
116   this study. Reviews list many such methods, often evaluated on the same classical wind
117   scenarios but with differing budgets, settings and constraint handling [5].
118       Common benchmarks. The GECCO layout competitions provided a common evalu-
119   ation interface, and the top entries of the 2015 edition were compared in [16]. The IEA
120   Wind Task 37 case studies fixed a simplified Gaussian wake, site boundaries and wind
121   roses [4, 42], and eight methods, gradient-based, gradient-free and hybrid, each run by
122   researchers experienced with it, reached different layouts of similar energy yield on a com-
123   mon problem [17]. Both efforts compare methods as submitted : each team chose its own
124   settings and effort, so the results show what experts achieve with their methods, not how
125   a given choice of baseline setting or control changes a ranking.
126       Gradient-based and model-based alternatives. Gradient-based optimization with exact
127   gradients reduces the cost of layout optimization considerably compared with finite differ-
128   ences [43]; pseudo-gradients offer a cheap alternative to exact gradients [44], and reduced

                                                   4
129   parameterizations of the layout cut the number of design variables [45]. Gaussian wake
130   models [46, 47] make the objective smoother than the top-hat Jensen wake, and surrogate
131   models extend layout optimization to larger farms and higher-fidelity flow models [48–50].
132       Position of this study. We use the Kusiak–Song benchmark in its continuous form
133   (discretized only in the wind directions), and test beyond it on a Horns Rev 1 block and
134   IEA37 Case Study 1 (Section 9). Unlike studies that propose a new method, we fix the
135   methods and vary the configuration and the controls; unlike competitions, every method
136   is run by the same code under one budget in objective evaluations, which for the gradient-
137   based baseline (multistart sequential least-squares programming, MS-SLSQP) includes its
138   finite-difference gradients. We do not test gradient-based methods on the smooth models
139   and with the exact gradients for which they are designed [17, 43].

140   2.2. Benchmarking Methodology for Metaheuristics
141       Hooker [10] distinguished competitive testing, which tells which algorithm wins, from
142   controlled experiments, which tell why. Later guidelines turned this into practice: equal
143   budgets counted in function evaluations, reported and justified settings of all methods,
144   well-chosen reference algorithms and component analyses of new proposals [12, 13]. Plat-
145   forms such as COCO [51] standardize budgets, instances and performance measures for
146   continuous black-box optimizers, but for artificial test functions; a constrained engineer-
147   ing problem such as the WFLOP must additionally decide how infeasible final solutions
148   enter the comparison.
149       For the statistics, Demšar [52] and Derrac et al. [11] recommended nonparametric
150   tests over multiple problems: the Friedman test with post hoc comparisons of average
151   ranks and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with a multiplicity correction such as
152   Holm’s [53]. Benavoli et al. showed that post hoc tests based on mean ranks depend on
153   the other methods in the pool [22], which is why our primary tests use case means, and
154   advocated Bayesian analyses with a region of practical equivalence [28]; Carrasco et al. [54]
155   reviewed these classical and newer trends for swarm and evolutionary algorithms and gave
156   practical guidelines. Campelo and Takahashi [55] derived the numbers of instances and
157   runs needed for a given power and accuracy. Equivalence testing with two one-sided tests
158   (TOST) asks whether a difference lies within a margin of practical irrelevance [23], and so
159   avoids reading a nonsignificant difference as evidence that two algorithms perform equally.
160       Position of this study. We apply this practice to the WFLOP and add three elements
161   that we have not found reported for this problem: feasibility-aware ranking of penalty-
162   based methods, equal-cost random-sampling controls for the first phase of hybrids, and
163   equivalence analyses (TOST and a Bayesian signed-rank test) at three levels of gener-
164   alization, over seeds, over cases and over farm clusters (Section 6). We did not size
165   the experiment by a power analysis as in [55]; instead, for each equivalence statement,
166   we report the smallest margin at which it holds, so that a reader can apply a different
167   margin.

168   2.3. Critiques of Metaphor-Based Algorithms
169       Sörensen [14] argued that many “novel” metaheuristics hide known search operators
170   behind a new metaphor and are justified by comparisons with weak baselines; a group of
171   editors and researchers later called for action on the proliferation of such methods [15].
172   Camacho-Villalón et al. [56] showed for six popular metaphor-based algorithms that their
173   search rules reuse known ideas under new names and are presented in a misleading way.
174   For the SSA [8], the critical review of Castelli et al. [57] analyzed the update rules and the


                                                    5
175   evidence for its performance; in the form used here, the leaders perturb the best solution
176   uniformly with a shrinking radius and the followers only average (Section 4.2).
177       Position of this study. These critiques rest mostly on the update equations or on
178   artificial test functions. We add a component-level test on an applied constrained problem:
179   whether a salp-swarm first phase does better than random sampling of equal cost in the
180   farm. It does not (Section 8). This is also a self-critique, as LX-SSA [26] was proposed
181   by two of the authors; the test assesses LX-SSA as published, including its cost of 2Np
182   evaluations per iteration, and does not separate its Laplace step from that cost.

183   2.4. PSO Parameterization and Stability Theory
184       The original PSO [18] used acceleration coefficients c1 = c2 = 2 and velocity clamp-
185   ing; the inertia weight [58] and the constriction factor of Clerc and Kennedy [59] were
186   introduced to control the swarm’s divergence, and the constriction setting is equivalent
187   to an inertia-weight PSO with w ≈ 0.73 and c1 = c2 ≈ 1.50. Analyses of the particle
188   dynamics [60, 61] derived the parameter regions in which a deterministic simplification
189   of a particle converges and related the parameters to the exploration behavior. Poli [19]
190   derived the mean and variance of the sampling distribution under stagnation and thereby
191   the order-1 and order-2 stability regions; Cleghorn and Engelbrecht [21] showed that,
192   for c1 = c2 , the order-2 region also holds under a weaker, non-stagnant assumption; and
193   Bonyadi and Michalewicz [20] reviewed these results together with the many PSO variants
194   and their parameter choices.
195       Position of this study. These results are well known in PSO theory but rarely used
196   when PSO serves as a baseline in applied comparisons. We use the order-2 condition as
197   a check of a baseline setting: the setting w = 0.7, c1 = c2 = 2 is order-1 but not order-2
198   stable (Proposition 1), a sweep of c1 = c2 shows that feasibility on the benchmark begins
199   to degrade near the boundary, and the setting changes the ranking of PSO against the
200   salp-swarm hybrids (Section 7.1). The link between a stability region of the unconstrained
201   dynamics and the feasibility of the returned solutions in a constrained problem is, to our
202   knowledge, not reported for the WFLOP.

203   2.5. Hybrid Swarm–Local-Search Designs
204       Combining a population-based global search with a local search is the idea of memetic
205   algorithms; Krasnogor and Smith [62] gave a taxonomy and discussed design issues such
206   as the choice of the local search and its integration into the evolutionary cycle, and Neri
207   and Cotta [27] reviewed memetic computing for discrete, continuous and constrained
208   problems. VNS [24, 25] changes neighborhoods systematically around an incumbent and
209   has a continuous form [63]; in the WFLOP, it has been used for large offshore farms [41],
210   and deterministic refinement techniques for layouts have been compared in [40]. The
211   simplest of these designs is the sequential (relay) hybrid, which runs the global method
212   for part of the budget and passes its best solution to the local method.
213       Position of this study. PSO-VNS is such a sequential hybrid, built from a standard
214   PSO and the basic VNS without new operators. What is new is its evaluation: the
215   first phase is compared with equal-cost random sampling in the farm and in its bounding
216   square, and with the local search alone, so that the contribution of each phase is measured
217   rather than assumed; and the second phase is instrumented, which shows that at 6,030
218   evaluations the VNS acts essentially as one compass-search descent, so the comparison
219   measures the value of a start for a local search (Section 8).

220   3. Problem Modeling
221        For a prescribed number N of identical turbines with rotor radius R (diameter D =
222   2R), the n = 2N coordinates of the layout X = (x1 , y1 , . . . , xN , yN ) are optimized to
223   maximize the expected farm power, i.e., to minimize the wake loss, under the Jensen wake
224   model [31, 32] and the power curve of [3]. The wind direction θ has the probability density
225   pθ ; it is the direction towards which the wind blows, counter-clockwise from east (Fig. 1a);
226   the meteorological directions θmet of Horns Rev 1 and IEA37 (wind from, clockwise from
227   north) are converted by θ = 270◦ − θmet . Two constraints apply (Section 3.4): the turbine
228   distances ℓij = ∥(xi − xj , yi − yj )∥2 are at least ℓmin = 4D = 8R [3, 33], and the farm is a
229   disc of radius r = 500, 750 or 1000 m.

230   3.1. Wind Model and Direction Discretization
231       For each direction, the wind speed s has the Weibull density ps (s; ζ, ψ) = (ζ/ψ)
232   (s/ψ)ζ−1 exp[−(s/ψ)ζ ] with shape ζ(θ) and scale ψ(θ). The benchmark rose is piecewise
233   constant: Nθ = 24 direction bins [15(l − 1), 15l)◦ , l = 1, . . . , 24, with probability pl , shape
234   ζ = 2 and scale ψl (Table S1). Data Set I is nearly unidirectional (ψ = 13 m/s in all bins;
235   80% of the probability in bins 6 and 7, i.e., wind from 165◦ to 195◦ ); Data Set II is a
236   broad rose with scales from 2.6 to 10 m/s and its highest probability in bin 13. The Horns
237   Rev 1 block uses a measured 12-sector Weibull rose evaluated in 5◦ bins, and IEA37 Case
238   Study 1 uses 16 directions at one speed (Section 6.3).
239       Why the direction discretization matters. The objective evaluates the wake only at
240   the 24 bin centers θ̄l = (15l − 7.5)◦ . A top-hat wake is narrow: by the half-cone geometry
241   (Fig. 1c), turbine j wakes a turbine i at√ the distance ρ ≥ R/K only for directions within
242   an interval of width 2[α + arcsin(R/(ρ 1 + K 2 ))] around the direction from j to i. With
243   K = 0.075 and R = 38.5 m (α = 4.29◦ , R/K = 513 m), this width is below the bin
244   spacing of 15◦ for ρ > 685 m and tends to 2α = 8.6◦ . If no bin center lies in this interval,
245   the wake of the pair does not enter the objective (with bins of 5◦ or finer, every such
246   interval contains a center). Optimized layouts exploit these gaps: with 1◦ sub-bins that
247   keep the Weibull parameters of their 15◦ bin, the mean wake loss of PSO-VNS rises from
248   1.322% to 2.335%, and the rise is largest for PSO and the VNS-based methods. All results
249   thus hold for the discretized roses; Section 10 reports which conclusions survive a 1◦ rose.

377   4.3. Basic Variable Neighborhood Search
378       The basic VNS [24, 25], in the continuous form of [63] (Algorithm 2), improves one
379   incumbent X with kmax = 5 ℓ∞ shell neighborhoods Nk (X) = {X′ : δk−1 < ∥X′ − X∥∞ ≤
380   δk }, δk = 0.1 k r, of the whole layout: shaking draws a uniform point of Nk (X), clipped
381   to the box, and a best-improvement local search improves it. As the top-hat wake makes
382   Fp discontinuous, the local search is a compass search: it evaluates all 2n moves ±h em
383   (em the mth unit vector; 60 evaluations for N = 15), moves to the best one if it strictly
384   improves, and halves h (from h0 = 0.05r) when none does, until h < hmin = 10−3 r.
385   A completed descent ends at a point that no move ±h̄ em , h̄ = h0 /25 = 0.0015625 r,
386   improves (Proposition S9), a coordinate-wise optimality at one step length, not a local
387   minimum.

388   5. Two-Phase Swarm–VNS Design and PSO-VNS
389   5.1. Two-Phase Template
390       All hybrids follow one sequential (relay) template. For a budget of B evaluations and
391   a split ω ∈ (0, 1), Phase 1 starts from the seeded initial population, runs a population
392   method for about ωB evaluations and passes its best layout to Phase 2, the basic VNS
393   (Algorithm 2), which applies the local search to it and then iterates until B evaluations
394   are used. Only Phase 1 differs: PSO in PSO-VNS ; SSA or LX-SSA in SSA-VNS and LX-
395   SSA-VNS (with ξ1 compressed to the Phase-1 iterations); and, in the controls, round(ωB)
396   layouts: the initial population, then layouts drawn uniformly in the farm disc (RSD-VNS )
397   or in its bounding square (RS-VNS ). VNS alone is the limiting case in which Phase 1
398   is the initial population. Phase 1 runs for T1 = round((ωB − Np )/c) iterations of c
399   evaluations (c = Np for PSO and SSA, 2Np for LX-SSA). A swarm phase is useful only
400   if it gives VNS a better start than random sampling with about the same number of
401   evaluations (at ω = 0.5, 3,015 for the controls and 3,030 for the swarms (Np (T1 + 1);
402   LX-SSA: Np (2T1 + 1))); the component analysis (Section 8) tests this for each Phase-1
403   choice against RSD-VNS, with RS-VNS as a weak minimum bar, as the following result

457       Equal budgets and paired seeds. Unless stated otherwise, a run uses exactly B =

498   with two one-sided tests (TOST) [23].

499   Definition 1 (Practical equivalence at three levels of inference). Let A and B be two
500   methods,    dc their case-mean difference over the n cases in which both are qualified, ∆L =
       1

      n    c dc , and m > 0 a margin. A and B are practically equivalent at margin m at a given
502   level if both one-sided hypotheses H0− : ∆ ≤ −m and H0+ : ∆ ≥ m about the estimand ∆
503   of that level are rejected at α = 0.05, i.e., if the 90% interval for ∆ lies inside (−m, m).
504   The three levels are:
505    (a) Seed level (the fixed benchmark). Estimand: the benchmark average of the expected
506        case-mean differences of the given benchmark cases; only the run-to-run variability is
507        random. Interval: percentile bootstrap that resamples the 30 seed-paired runs within
508        every case (the same seeds for both methods; case means over the feasible resampled
509        runs).
510    (b) Case level (exchangeable cases). Estimand: the expected case-mean difference over a
511        population of cases like the 68; the cases are treated as independent draws. Interval:
512        percentile bootstrap over the cases.
513    (c) Cluster level (new farms). Estimand: the expected difference over new (data set,
514        radius) clusters; the 68 cases form six clusters with nested N . Interval: cluster-
515        robust t interval with the bias-reduced CR2 variance and a t distribution with G−1 =
516        5 degrees of freedom (G = 6 clusters), and a restricted wild-cluster bootstrap-t TOST
517        (CR2-studentized, all 66 draws of Webb’s six-point weights enumerated); equivalence
518        at this level requires both.
519   The minimal margin of a level, mmin = max(|ℓ|, |u|) for the 90% interval [ℓ, u], is the
520   smallest margin at which equivalence holds: it holds for every m > mmin and for none
521   smaller.

522       TOST has size at most α without a multiplicity correction (intersection–union prin-
523   ciple; Section S6.4). The estimate ∆L is the same at every level, but the interval widens
524   from the seed to the cluster level, so mmin grows and an equivalence statement must name
525   its level (Section 7.3). “A gives no gain larger than m over B” (non-superiority) needs
526   only one of the two tests: the lower end of the 90% interval must lie above −m. A re-
527   sult that is neither equivalent nor significantly different is inconclusive and supports no
528   statement of the kind “as good as”. The margin m = 0.05 pp (4.1 and 2.1 MWh/yr per
529   turbine in Data Sets I and II) is a post hoc convention; the minimal margins mmin let
530   readers apply their own.
531       Bayesian signed-rank test. The Bayesian signed-rank test [28] places a Dirichlet-
532   process prior (strength 0.5, pseudo-observation at 0) on the distribution of the dc and,
533   for every posterior sample, computes the shares θA , θrope and θB of the Walsh averages
534   (dc + dc′ )/2 below −m, inside the region of practical equivalence (ROPE) [−m, m] and
535   above m. We report the posterior probability that equivalence is the most probable of
536   the three outcomes, the share of the 50,000 posterior samples in which θrope is the largest

537   (Section 8.3 and Table S25). It is not the probability that ∆ lies within ±m; it concerns

724   8.1. The VNS Phase
725       The VNS phase improves both salp-swarm methods: SSA-VNS is 39/29/0 (W/T/L)
726   against SSA and LX-SSA-VNS 34/34/0 against LX-SSA (∆L = −0.294 and −0.394). For

730   PSO-VNS, the shake-and-search cycles take 21.5% of the Phase-2 evaluations (pooled;
731   median run 4.7%) but give 0.5% of the Phase-2 gain (2.5% with the cycle cut off by the
732   budget), and the median run with N ≥ 12 never shakes (Table S50). The analysis thus
733   measures the value of a local-search phase.
734       In Table S50 (12 split cases × 10 seeds), the first descent from the switch point takes
735   a median of 2,860 evaluations in PSO-VNS and gives 97.5% of the Phase-2 improvement
736   (SSA-VNS 90.3%, RS-VNS 88.6%). A shake costs one evaluation, but the new descent
737   after it scans 2n = 4N moves per step, and 114 of 120 runs accept no cycle; accepted
738   cycles use only k = 1. For N ≥ 12 the first descent is unfinished in 39 of 40 runs, so the
739   local-minimality guarantee (Proposition S9) does not apply there. VNS never makes a
740   feasible incumbent infeasible and starts from the best feasible Phase-1 layout when one
741   exists. The contrasts thus test a truncated compass search after Phase 1; shaking and
742   neighborhood change can matter only for small N .

743   8.2. Random-Sampling Controls
744       Random sampling of equal cost replaces Phase 1, uniform in the site disc (RSD-VNS)

747   circle; the spacing constraint is binding, and disc sampling rescues only 242 of these runs.
748   RSD-VNS beats RS-VNS (6/62/0, ∆L = −0.093) and ranks third (3.77).

750   square samples wholly inside the circle matches (π/4)N (Proposition 2), but the feasible
751   share vanishes much sooner, as random positions rarely keep all N (N − 1)/2 pairs ℓmin
752   apart: for N = 15 in the 1000-m farm, 2.64% of the 90,450 samples lie inside and none
753   is feasible. The 730 runs without a feasible sample (including all runs of 14 cases) start
754   VNS from the least-penalized sample; paired by seed, 242 of them would get a feasible
755   sample in the disc and 488 none. RS-VNS is thus weak mainly because of the spacing,
756   only in part because of the square, and beating it is only a minimum bar.
757       In the disc, the feasible Phase-1 samples rise from 9.1% to 19.3% and the runs without
758   one fall from 730 to 494. At the switch (Table S14; controls read at 3,000 evaluations),

923   10. Sensitivity to the Modeling Assumptions
924       The benchmark fixes a power curve, a wake model, a minimum spacing, a boundary
925   rule and a discretization of the wind rose. We re-evaluated every feasible final layout of
926   the main comparison, without re-optimization, under a cubic power curve, a Gaussian
927   wake and finer direction bins (Table 12; all methods: Table S36); this asks whether the
928   delivered layouts keep their order under another model, not which layouts the methods
929   would find for it.

930   10.1. Power Curve
931       A cubic power curve between cut-in and rated speed [69] (also with a 25 m/s cut-out)
932   lowers the expected power by 17.7% (Data Set I) and 36.9% (Data Set II), so our energy
933   values are benchmark values, but it leaves the ranking intact (τ̄ = 0.95; same best method
934   in 94% of the cases; PSO-VNS first, also with the cut-out).

935   10.2. Wake Model
936       The uncalibrated Gaussian wake of [46] (KG = 0.04; Section S5.1) differs from the
937   Jensen cone (K = 0.075) in its smooth lateral profile and expansion rate and, unlike the
938   cone test of the benchmark, gives no deficit to turbines upstream of the wake source.
939   The mean objective changes little (Table 12), but the wake loss of the same final layouts
940   differs between the two models by 0.38 pp on average (median 0.27 pp), 7.6 times the
941   equivalence margin (Section 6.2), and by more than the margin for 86% of the 15,614
942   layouts, whereas the per-case difference PSO-VNS − PSO changes by only 0.031 pp on
943   average. The ordering is less stable (τ̄ = 0.66), and PSO is narrowly first (1.91 vs. 1.97);
944   with 1◦ direction bins, PSO-VNS is first under the Gaussian wake as well (Section 10.5).



                                                                31
945   10.3. Minimum Spacing
946       The optimized layouts often lie on the 4D constraint: 49% of the feasible final layouts
947   also satisfy 5D and 36% 6D (4.1% and 0.1% for N = 11–15, where the fixed site leaves less
948   room), so they do not transfer to larger spacings without re-optimization (site capacity:
949   Table S37). PSO-VNS and PSO were not re-run at larger spacings (archived SSA and
950   LX-SSA runs: Table S38).

951   10.4. Boundary Rule
952       All methods clip the coordinates to the bounding square and leave the circle to the
953   penalty (Section 3.4). The rule matters: an SSA that projects turbines radially onto the
954   circle instead attains higher mean objectives than the original SSA in all six representative
955   cases (p ≤ 0.0036), as the projection returns a turbine to the site directly. All methods
956   therefore use the same rule: the comparison is one of search strategies under a common
957   repair.

958   10.5. Direction Resolution
959       The benchmark evaluates the wake only at the centers of 24 direction bins of 15◦ , so a
960   pair of turbines can be placed with no bin center in its narrow wake interval (Section 3).
961   We split each bin into 3 (5◦ ) or 15 (1◦ ) sub-bins with its Weibull parameters and an
962   equal share of its frequency (wake-free power unchanged) and re-evaluated all 21,716
963   feasible final layouts (Table S39). The 1◦ rose raises the mean wake loss of every method,
964   by 0.40 pp (DE) to 1.04 pp (PSO; PSO-VNS: 1.322% to 2.335%), mostly already with
965   5◦ sub-bins: optimized layouts exploit the gaps between the coarse bins, those of PSO
966   and the VNS-based methods most. PSO-VNS still ranks first (2.09; PSO 2.37, the only
967   method not significantly different), also under the Gaussian wake at 1◦ (2.22 vs. 2.35);
968   PSO’s narrow lead under the Gaussian wake with 15◦ bins (1.91 vs. 1.97; τ̄ = 0.66) is
969   a property of the coarse rose. Beyond the top two the order changes (τ̄ = 0.52; same
970   best method in 37% of the cases): MS-SLSQP moves from sixth to third, and its gap to
971   PSO-VNS shrinks from 0.435 to 0.135 pp but remains significant.
972       For PSO-VNS vs. PSO (Table S39; details: Table S40), the case-mean difference
973   becomes −0.043 pp (90% CI [−0.062, −0.025]): a small but significant advantage (p =
974   2.5 × 10−4 ), not equivalence at ±0.05 pp (minimal margin 0.062 pp); the same holds with
975   5◦ sub-bins. The sign of the per-case difference changes in 26 of the 60 cases with a
976   nonzero difference, so the per-case winner often depends on the discretization. On the 44
977   non-trivial cases, equivalence is shown at neither resolution, and the Data Set II N ≥ 10
978   advantage remains (10 of 10 cases). The component-analysis conclusions hold, but the
979   small SSA gain over RS-VNS is no longer significant (Table S41). The order beyond the
980   top two and the equivalence verdict are thus properties of the discretized benchmark; the
981   first place of PSO-VNS is not.

982   11. Discussion
983       In this benchmark, which method appears best depends strongly on the protocol; we
984   discuss why, what follows for hybrid design and layout practice, and condense the protocol
985   into a checklist.




                                                   32
 986   11.1. What Drives the Conclusions of Metaheuristic Comparisons
 987       Baseline configuration. An advantage over PSO is informative only if the PSO base-
 988   line is convergent: the old setting lies beyond the order-2 boundary (11), which marks
 989   approximately where this PSO starts to fail, and places PSO at position 5 of Table 3
 990   instead of 1 (Section 7.1). The c-sweep links this to Proposition 1 without establishing a
 991   cause (feasibility starts to decline in the step containing c∗ = 1.7486), and velocity clamp-
 992   ing largely restores feasibility (92.2%), whereas zeroing clipped components does not: the
 993   setting fails mainly through large steps. The stability region is thus a cheap screen for
 994   settings, not a guarantee.
 995       Controls. An advantage of a hybrid’s first phase is informative only if it beats random
 996   sampling in the farm at equal cost. Square sampling is too weak a control (730 RS-VNS
 997   runs draw no feasible sample, mainly because of the spacing), so a salp-swarm phase
 998   appears to help against it; against disc sampling only the PSO phase gains, and the small
 999   SSA gain over RS-VNS is no longer significant with 1◦ bins (Section 8). With essentially
1000   one compass-search descent as second phase, this measures the value of a start [36].
1001       Level of equivalence. Non-significance is not equivalence, and equivalence depends on
1002   the level and the evaluation: PSO-VNS and PSO are equivalent at ±0.05 pp across seeds
1003   and cases but not across farm clusters, nor under a 1◦ rose, where PSO-VNS has a small
1004   significant advantage (Sections 7.3 and 10). The levels answer different questions (this
1005   benchmark, similar cases, new farms), and the minimal margin grows with them (0.026,
1006   0.041 and 0.067 pp); equivalence holds for N < 10 but not for N ≥ 10, and 19 of 68 cases
1007   differ by more than the margin, in both directions.
1008       Discretization, initialization and budget. With 1◦ sub-bins the wake loss of every
1009   method rises (PSO-VNS: 1.322% to 2.335%), as optimized layouts exploit the gaps be-
1010   tween the 15◦ bins (Section 10). PSO-VNS and PSO keep the first two places, but
1011   MS-SLSQP moves from sixth to third and PSO’s lead under the Gaussian wake with 15◦
1012   bins disappears. From feasible starts, a single local search (VNS alone) ranks first on the
1013   six largest cases (Section 9.2), and at 120,030 evaluations the leading methods lie within
1014   0.17 pp (Section 9.1). A ranking is thus the ranking of a protocol.

1015   11.2. Implications for the Design of Two-Phase Swarm–Local-Search Hybrids
1016       The swarm phase as a feasibility-reaching start. A first phase need not be a good
1017   optimizer, only a cheap source of good starts; in our runs, the PSO phase is valuable
1018   largely because it reaches feasibility: at the switch, its global best is feasible in 98.7% of
1019   the runs, against 95.7% for SSA and 75.7% for disc sampling, whereas feasible disc samples
1020   are not worse (wake loss 1.53% vs. 1.63%, over different runs). From feasible starts, PSO
1021   never improves on the best initial layout and VNS alone ranks first (Section 9.2). A
1022   swarm phase is thus most useful when random layouts are infeasible; when a feasible start
1023   is cheap (our packing initialization costs no objective evaluations), the budget is better
1024   spent on the local search.
1025       The local-search phase and the split. At 6,030 evaluations, the first compass-search
1026   descent gives 97.5% of the Phase-2 gain and is unfinished in 39 of 40 runs with N ≥ 12
1027   (Section 8). At small budgets the cost of the descent (2n evaluations per step) thus
1028   matters more than the neighborhoods, which act only for small N ; cheaper descents are
1029   obvious candidates, but untested. The first descent also repairs infeasible switch points
1030   (densest cases: 63.3% feasible at the switch, all at the end). Of the splits tested, ω = 0.75
1031   was best and plain PSO (ω = 1) had a higher mean loss (Section 8.4): the swarm needs
1032   enough evaluations to reach feasibility, and the descent enough to progress.

                                                    33
1033       Where PSO-VNS helps and where it does not. At 6,030 evaluations from random
1034   starts, PSO-VNS reaches feasibility more reliably on the Horns Rev 1 block (30/30 vs.
1035   19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 ; on the benchmark PSO is feasible in every run
1036   as well), ranks best on the six largest cases at all three budgets (exploratory), and, in
1037   the post hoc subgroup of the larger Data Set II layouts (N ≥ 10), has the lower mean
1038   wake loss in 10 of 10 cases (pHolm ≤ 0.018), also with 1◦ bins. It does not help from
1039   feasible starts, in the densest Data Set I layouts (all 7 Data Set I cases beyond the margin
1040   favor PSO), on IEA37, or on the benchmark average by more than a few hundredths of a
1041   percentage point.

1042   11.3. Implications for Wind Farm Layout Practice
1043       The direction discretization is the main caveat for practice: with 1◦ bins the benchmark
1044   wake loss rises by 0.40–1.04 pp, and none of the 901 feasible optimized Horns Rev 1 layouts
1045   exceeds the installed block (16 with the 5◦ bins used in optimization). Gains of layouts
1046   optimized under a coarse rose should be verified at a fine resolution before they are
1047   credited to an optimizer.
1048       The Horns Rev 1 block, without the other 64 turbines, with 4D instead of 7D spacing
1049   and without cables, loads or navigation, tests optimizers, not the installed design (Sec-
1050   tion 9.3). Our model takes CT at the free-stream speed (wake loss −0.72 pp relative to
1051   PyWake’s NOJ model); with CT at the local speed it reproduces PyWake’s hub-center
1052   NOJ exactly, as design studies require. On IEA37, the best PSO-VNS layouts at 30,030
1053   evaluations lie 1.93% (16 turbines) and 6.23% (36 turbines) below the best feasible pub-
1054   lished layouts in AEP; they came from the participants’ own optimizers, some gradient-
1055   based, with budgets not reported comparably [4, 17]. The methods compared here, at
1056   these budgets, are reference points for comparing optimizers, not design tools.

1060   12. Threats to Validity
1061       Internal validity. PSO-VNS repeats PSO exactly up to the switch, and the controls
1062   differ from the hybrids only in Phase 1 (3,015 versus 3,030 evaluations); the code repro-
1063   duces the original and stored runs (Section 6.4). Configuration remains a threat: DE is
1064   untuned (its low feasibility, 71.4%, may reflect penalty-dominated search), MS-SLSQP
1065   uses finite differences on a discontinuous objective, and the old PSO setting was run
1066   without velocity clamping, which largely restores its feasibility, and with one boundary
1067   rule and penalty scaling. The SSA–LX-SSA contrast confounds the Laplace step with the
1068   doubled cost per iteration.
1069       Construct validity. At 6,030 evaluations the “VNS” phase is essentially one truncated
1070   compass-search descent, so the component analysis concerns this local search, not VNS in
1071   general. The objective, the gross energy of a discretized rose under a hub-center top-hat
1072   Jensen wake, is a benchmark value, not farm quality; final quality at a fixed budget is
1073   the only measure tested; and the feasibility-first ranking limits, but does not remove,
1074   survivorship bias.
1075       External validity. All results are for discretized roses (15◦ benchmark bins, 5◦ on
1076   Horns Rev 1), which optimized layouts exploit, a top-hat Jensen wake, circular farms, a

1091   13. Conclusions and Future Work
1092       On the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark with random starts and 6,030 evaluations,
1093   three findings emerge. First, the baseline configuration changes the conclusions: the old
1094   PSO setting, which lies beyond the order-2 boundary and rarely samples feasible layouts
1095   late in the run, puts PSO behind salp-swarm hybrids, whereas the constriction setting puts
1096   it ahead of every salp-swarm method at this budget. Second, salp-swarm first phases add
1097   nothing beyond random sampling in the farm (a gain above the margin is ruled out across
1098   seeds and cases; LX-SSA as published is worse), whereas a PSO phase gives the second
1099   phase, essentially one compass-search descent, a clearly better start. Third, PSO-VNS
1100   has the best average rank with 15◦ , 5◦ and 1◦ direction bins, also under a Gaussian wake
1101   at 1◦ , but its average advantage over a well-configured PSO is small: within ±0.05 pp
1102   under the benchmark rose (across seeds and cases, not farm clusters) and −0.043 pp under

                                                        35
1103   a 1◦ rose, with case-level differences in both directions that follow a data set × density
1104   pattern (exploratory). PSO-VNS reaches feasibility more reliably on Horns Rev 1 (30/30
1105   vs. 19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 ); it is a consistent reference method, not a
1106   superior optimizer.
1107       Future work follows from the threats above. Expensive wake models: surrogate-assisted
1108   evaluation (graph neural networks mapping a layout and a wind state to turbine powers,
1109   or data-driven and Kriging surrogates [48–50]) would make equal-budget comparisons at
1110   higher fidelity affordable, if the surrogate error is reported next to the method differences.
1111   Larger farms, such as the IEA37 64-turbine scenario and the full 80-turbine Horns Rev 1.
1112   Exact-gradient baselines on a smooth wake model [17], instead of finite differences on
1113   a discontinuous objective. Stronger baselines and controls: CMA-ES, L-SHADE, tuned
1114   DE, a cost-matched ablation of the Laplace step, and random-sampling controls at larger
1115   budgets. Finer roses during optimization, with loads and economic objectives.
