Abstract
We compare eight wind farm layout optimizers on the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark
(68 cases, 15◦ rose) with 6,030 evaluations from random starts, feasibility-aware ranking
and equivalence tests. First, the particle swarm optimization (PSO) setting w = 0.7,
c1 = c2 = 2 lies beyond the order-2 (variance) stability boundary; in a sweep of c1 = c2 ,
feasibility starts to degrade there and falls progressively beyond it. With it, PSO ranks
behind both salp-swarm hybrids in the eight-method pool of a preliminary, unpublished
comparison of ours; with the constriction setting, it ranks first there. Second, a salp-
swarm (SSA) first phase adds nothing beyond equal-cost random sampling in the farm (a
gain above 0.05 percentage points (pp) of wake loss is ruled out across seeds and cases),
the Laplacian SSA as published is worse, and a PSO phase gives a clear gain; at this
budget the second phase, variable neighborhood search (VNS), acts essentially as one
compass-search descent. Third, PSO followed by VNS (PSO-VNS) has the best average
rank with 15◦ , 5◦ and 1◦ direction bins, also under a Gaussian wake at 1◦ , but its average
advantage over a well-configured PSO is small: within ±0.05 pp across seeds and cases,
not farm clusters, under the 15◦ rose, and a significant −0.043 pp under a 1◦ rose; case-
level differences go both ways. PSO-VNS reaches feasibility more reliably (Horns Rev 1
block: 30/30 vs. 19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 ).
Keywords: Benchmarking, equivalence testing, particle swarm optimization,
random-sampling control, wake effect, wind farm layout optimization

 3   1. Introduction
 4       The positions of the turbines in a wind farm are fixed at the planning stage and
 5   determine, for its lifetime, how much of the available wind energy it converts. An upstream
 6   turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of
 7   the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the gross energy
 8   yield, and shapes the collector system and grid connection [1, 2]. As the wake loss is
 9   a share of the wake-free energy, a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point is,
10   within a given wake model, a lifetime energy difference of that size. The wind farm layout
11   optimization problem (WFLOP) places a given number of turbines in a site to minimize
12   the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints; we address only this
13   wake-loss part of layout design, for farms of up to 36 turbines.
14       For swarm and evolutionary computation, the continuous WFLOP is also a represen-
15   tative constrained benchmark. Its 2N coordinates are continuous, the objective is multi-
16   modal and, with a top-hat wake, discontinuous; the constraints are a site boundary and
17   N (N − 1)/2 pairwise spacing constraints, so the feasible region is nonconvex and becomes
18   a small part of the bounding box as N grows; one evaluation is cheap enough for thou-
19   sands of runs; and standard instances with published results exist, from the Kusiak–Song
20   cases [3] to the IEA Wind Task 37 case studies [4]. These properties, which it shares with
21   many engineering design problems, have made it a common test bed for new metaheuris-
22   tics [5], including swarm methods such as particle swarm optimization (PSO) [6, 7] and,
23   more recently, metaphor-based algorithms such as the salp swarm algorithm (SSA) [8, 9].
24   What such studies can show depends on how the comparison is run.
25       A new algorithm is usually justified by a comparison with baselines, and its claimed
26   advantage is only as reliable as that comparison. Guidelines for experiments with meta-
27   heuristics [10–13] and critiques of metaphor-based algorithms [14, 15] both point to the
28   same weak spots: baselines run with poor or unreported settings, budgets that are not
29   equal, constraint violations that are ignored in the ranking, and statistics that confuse the
30   absence of a significant difference with equivalence. In the WFLOP, the GECCO layout
31   competition [16] and the IEA Wind Task 37 studies [4, 17] compared optimizers on a
32   common objective, and reviews list many metaheuristics tested under differing budgets
33   and settings [5]; these studies compare methods as submitted or as published, not how
34   the configuration of a baseline or the choice of control changes which method appears

45       Our thesis is that the conclusions of such comparisons depend strongly on how the
46   baselines are configured and how the comparison is controlled. Under a controlled proto-
47   col on the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark at 6,030 evaluations from random starts,

55     1. Protocol. Established practice [11–13] applied to the continuous WFLOP (equal
56        budgets counting gradient evaluations, seed-paired runs, Holm-corrected tests, pub-
57        lic code and data), with three elements that, to our knowledge, have not been applied
58        in WFLOP comparisons: feasibility-aware ranking (standard in constrained evolu-
59        tionary computation [28, 29]), equal-cost random-sampling controls for hybrids, and

62        a Horns Rev 1 block and IEA37 Case Study 1 (Sections 6 and 9). The protocol is not
63        specific to the WFLOP and carries over to other constrained continuous problems
64        solved by penalty-based swarm and evolutionary methods.

66        clamping) lies beyond the order-2 boundary (Proposition 1); in a sweep of c1 = c2 ,
67        feasibility starts to degrade at the boundary and falls progressively beyond it, and
68        the setting reverses the order of PSO and the salp-swarm hybrids (Section 7.1). A
69        stability condition from PSO theory thus serves as a practical check of a baseline
70        before a comparison is run.
71     3. Component analysis. Against random sampling in the farm (RSD-VNS), salp-swarm

 97   2. Related Work
 98       We review five strands of work and state, for each, what is new here; no new optimizer
 99   is proposed.

100   2.1. Wind Farm Layout Optimization with Metaheuristics
101        Discrete and continuous formulations. Early studies placed turbines in the cells of
102   a square grid and optimized the cell occupation with genetic algorithms (GAs) [31, 32];
103   binary PSO with time-varying acceleration coefficients was applied to the same kind of
104   grid [6]. A grid makes the problem combinatorial but restricts the layouts that can be
105   found. Kusiak and Song [3] formulated a continuous model with turbine coordinates in
106   a circular farm, a discretized wind rose, a Jensen wake [33, 34] and explicit boundary
107   and spacing constraints. This model, with two wind data sets [3, 35] and several farm
108   radii, has become a common reference for metaheuristics and defines the benchmark used
109   here (Section 6). On it, or on close variants, ant colony optimization (ACO) and particle
110   filtering [36, 37], biogeography-based optimization (BBO) [35] and random search inside
111   the feasible space [38] have been proposed, and GA, PSO, differential evolution (DE) and
112   simulated annealing were compared on offshore layouts [39]; PSO has reduced wake losses
113   in [7].
114        Newer metaheuristics and hybrids. Later work used self-informed GAs [40], an adap-
115   tive evolutionary algorithm combined with Monte Carlo tree search [41], deterministic
116   refinement techniques [42] and VNS for large offshore farms [43]. Metaphor-based algo-
117   rithms have also been applied; for example, the water cycle algorithm was compared with
118   the SSA and the grey wolf optimizer on turbine placement [9], and the Laplacian SSA of
119   two of the authors [26] was combined with VNS in a preliminary, unpublished compari-
120   son of ours that motivated this study. Reviews list many such methods, often evaluated
121   on the same classical wind scenarios but with differing budgets, settings and constraint
122   handling [5].
123        Common benchmarks. The GECCO layout competitions provided a common evalu-
124   ation interface, and the top entries of the 2015 edition were compared in [16]. The IEA
125   Wind Task 37 case studies fixed a simplified Gaussian wake, site boundaries and wind
126   roses [4, 44], and eight methods, gradient-based, gradient-free and hybrid, each run by



                                                  4
127   researchers experienced with it, reached different layouts of similar energy yield on a com-
128   mon problem [17]. Both efforts compare methods as submitted : each team chose its own
129   settings and effort, so the results show what experts achieve with their methods, not how
130   a given choice of baseline setting or control changes a ranking.
131       Gradient-based and model-based alternatives. Gradient-based optimization with exact
132   gradients reduces the cost of layout optimization considerably compared with finite differ-
133   ences [45]; pseudo-gradients offer a cheap alternative to exact gradients [46], and reduced
134   parameterizations of the layout cut the number of design variables [47]. Gaussian wake
135   models [48, 49] make the objective smoother than the top-hat Jensen wake. On such
136   smooth models, the cost of a gradient decides how far these methods scale: a forward-
137   difference gradient of the 2N coordinates costs 2N + 1 objective evaluations, whereas an
138   analytic or reverse-mode algorithmically differentiated gradient costs a small multiple of
139   one evaluation, at the price of memory [50]. With algorithmic differentiation, parallel
140   evaluation of the flow cases and a heuristic initial layout, gradient-based optimization
141   has been applied to farms of 500 turbines [50]; stochastic gradient descent reduces the
142   cost of each step by sampling the wind conditions [51]; and a heuristic start followed by
143   gradient-based refinement handles inclusion and exclusion zones [52]. Surrogate models
144   extend layout optimization to larger farms and higher-fidelity flow models [53–55]. Graph
145   neural networks, which represent a farm as a graph of interacting turbines and thus apply
146   to any layout, have been trained to predict turbine powers [56] and wake flow fields [57],
147   and have served as surrogates in layout [56] and yaw optimization [58]. With large-eddy
148   simulations as the flow model, Bayesian optimization with Gaussian-process surrogates
149   has optimized layouts and wake steering without gradients [59].
150       Position of this study. We use the Kusiak–Song benchmark in its continuous form
151   (discretized only in the wind directions), and test beyond it on a Horns Rev 1 block
152   and IEA37 Case Study 1 (Section 9). Unlike studies that propose a new method, we
153   fix the methods and vary the configuration and the controls; unlike competitions, every
154   method is run by the same code under one budget in objective evaluations, which for the
155   gradient-based baseline (multistart sequential least-squares programming, MS-SLSQP)
156   includes its finite-difference gradients. Exact-gradient and surrogate-based methods are
157   outside the present pool: they need a smooth model (the top-hat objective used here is
158   discontinuous) or training data, and each is reported under its own budget; we do not
159   test gradient-based methods on the smooth models and with the exact gradients for which
160   they are designed [17, 45]. The protocol is complementary to them: budgets that count
161   every model evaluation, including gradient and training evaluations, seed-paired runs and
162   equal-cost controls apply in the same way; for instance, a heuristic initial layout [50, 52]
163   could be compared with random sampling of equal cost in the farm, as the first phases
164   are here.

165   2.2. Benchmarking Methodology for Metaheuristics
166       Hooker [10] distinguished competitive testing, which tells which algorithm wins, from
167   controlled experiments, which tell why. Later guidelines turned this into practice: equal
168   budgets counted in function evaluations, reported and justified settings of all methods,
169   well-chosen reference algorithms and component analyses of new proposals [12, 13]. Plat-
170   forms such as COCO [60] standardize budgets, instances and performance measures for
171   continuous black-box optimizers, but for artificial test functions; a constrained engineer-
172   ing problem such as the WFLOP must additionally decide how infeasible final solutions
173   enter the comparison.


                                                   5
174       For the statistics, Demšar [61] and Derrac et al. [11] recommended nonparametric
175   tests over multiple problems: the Friedman test with post hoc comparisons of average
176   ranks and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with a multiplicity correction such as
177   Holm’s [62]. Benavoli et al. showed that post hoc tests based on mean ranks depend on
178   the other methods in the pool [22], which is why our primary tests use case means, and
179   advocated Bayesian analyses with a region of practical equivalence [30]; Carrasco et al. [63]
180   reviewed these classical and newer trends for swarm and evolutionary algorithms and gave
181   practical guidelines. Campelo and Takahashi [64] derived the numbers of instances and
182   runs needed for a given power and accuracy. Equivalence testing with two one-sided tests
183   (TOST) asks whether a difference lies within a margin of practical irrelevance [23], and so
184   avoids reading a nonsignificant difference as evidence that two algorithms perform equally.
185       Position of this study. We apply this practice to the WFLOP and add three elements
186   that we have not found applied in WFLOP comparisons: feasibility-aware ranking of
187   penalty-based methods, which follows the feasibility rules of constrained evolutionary com-
188   putation [28] and the feasibility-first ranking of the CEC constrained competitions [29];
189   equal-cost random-sampling controls for the first phase of hybrids; and equivalence anal-
190   yses (TOST and a Bayesian signed-rank test) at three levels of generalization, over seeds,
191   over cases and over farm clusters (Section 6). We did not size the experiment by a power
192   analysis as in [64]; instead, for each equivalence statement, we report the smallest margin
193   at which it holds, so that a reader can apply a different margin.

194   2.3. Critiques of Metaphor-Based Algorithms
195       Sörensen [14] argued that many “novel” metaheuristics hide known search operators
196   behind a new metaphor and are justified by comparisons with weak baselines; a group of
197   editors and researchers later called for action on the proliferation of such methods [15].
198   Camacho-Villalón et al. [65] showed for six popular metaphor-based algorithms that their
199   search rules reuse known ideas under new names and are presented in a misleading way.
200   For the SSA [8], the critical review of Castelli et al. [66] analyzed the update rules and the
201   evidence for its performance; in the form used here, the leaders perturb the best solution
202   uniformly with a shrinking radius and the followers only average (Section 4.2).
203       Position of this study. These critiques rest mostly on the update equations or on
204   artificial test functions. We add a component-level test on an applied constrained problem:
205   whether a salp-swarm first phase does better than random sampling of equal cost in the
206   farm. It does not (Section 8). This is also a self-critique, as LX-SSA [26] was proposed
207   by two of the authors; the test assesses LX-SSA as published, including its cost of 2Np
208   evaluations per iteration, and does not separate its Laplace step from that cost.

209   2.4. PSO Parameterization and Stability Theory
210       The original PSO [18] used acceleration coefficients c1 = c2 = 2 and velocity clamp-
211   ing; the inertia weight [67] and the constriction factor of Clerc and Kennedy [68] were
212   introduced to control the swarm’s divergence, and the constriction setting is equivalent
213   to an inertia-weight PSO with w ≈ 0.73 and c1 = c2 ≈ 1.50. Analyses of the particle
214   dynamics [69, 70] derived the parameter regions in which a deterministic simplification
215   of a particle converges and related the parameters to the exploration behavior. Poli [19]
216   derived the mean and variance of the sampling distribution under stagnation and thereby
217   the order-1 and order-2 stability regions; Cleghorn and Engelbrecht [21] showed that,
218   for c1 = c2 , the order-2 region also holds under a weaker, non-stagnant assumption; and
219   Bonyadi and Michalewicz [20] reviewed these results together with the many PSO variants
220   and their parameter choices.

                                                    6
221       Position of this study. These results are well known in PSO theory but rarely used
222   when PSO serves as a baseline in applied comparisons. We use the order-2 condition as
223   a check of a baseline setting: the setting w = 0.7, c1 = c2 = 2 is order-1 but not order-2
224   stable (Proposition 1), a sweep of c1 = c2 shows that feasibility on the benchmark begins
225   to degrade near the boundary, and the setting changes the ranking of PSO against the
226   salp-swarm hybrids (Section 7.1). The link between a stability region of the unconstrained
227   dynamics and the feasibility of the returned solutions in a constrained problem is, to our
228   knowledge, not reported for the WFLOP.

229   2.5. Hybrid Swarm–Local-Search Designs
230       Combining a population-based global search with a local search is the idea of memetic
231   algorithms; Krasnogor and Smith [71] gave a taxonomy and discussed design issues such
232   as the choice of the local search and its integration into the evolutionary cycle, and Neri
233   and Cotta [27] reviewed memetic computing for discrete, continuous and constrained
234   problems. VNS [24, 25] changes neighborhoods systematically around an incumbent and
235   has a continuous form [72]; in the WFLOP, it has been used for large offshore farms [43],
236   and deterministic refinement techniques for layouts have been compared in [42]. The
237   simplest of these designs is the sequential (relay) hybrid, which runs the global method
238   for part of the budget and passes its best solution to the local method.
239       Position of this study. PSO-VNS is such a sequential hybrid, built from a standard
240   PSO and the basic VNS without new operators. What is new is its evaluation: the
241   first phase is compared with equal-cost random sampling in the farm and in its bounding
242   square, and with the local search alone, so that the contribution of each phase is measured
243   rather than assumed; and the second phase is instrumented, which shows that at 6,030
244   evaluations the VNS acts essentially as one compass-search descent, so the comparison
245   measures the value of a start for a local search (Section 8).

246   3. Problem Modeling
247        For a prescribed number N of identical turbines with rotor radius R (diameter D =
248   2R), the n = 2N coordinates of the layout X = (x1 , y1 , . . . , xN , yN ) are optimized to
249   maximize the expected farm power, i.e., to minimize the wake loss, under the Jensen wake
250   model [33, 34] and the power curve of [3]. The wind direction θ has the probability density
251   pθ ; it is the direction towards which the wind blows, counter-clockwise from east (Fig. 1a);
252   the meteorological directions θmet of Horns Rev 1 and IEA37 (wind from, clockwise from
253   north) are converted by θ = 270◦ − θmet . Two constraints apply (Section 3.4): the turbine
254   distances ℓij = ∥(xi − xj , yi − yj )∥2 are at least ℓmin = 4D = 8R [3, 35], and the farm is a
255   disc of radius r = 500, 750 or 1000 m. The spacing 4D is the convention of the Kusiak–
256   Song benchmark and is kept for comparability; it lies at the dense end of practice (the
257   installed Horns Rev 1 layout is a regular grid of about 7D; Section 9.3), so our results
258   concern a packing-constrained regime, and Section 10.3 reports how far the optimized
259   layouts also satisfy larger spacings.
260        The model is the unchanged Kusiak–Song benchmark model, and no modeling novelty
261   is claimed; the contribution of this study is the comparison protocol. The terrain is flat,
262   and the electrical collector-system layout and multi-objective formulations (e.g., AEP
263   against cost or the levelized cost of energy) are not considered. The protocol itself (equal
264   budgets, seed pairing, equal-cost controls, feasibility-aware ranking and equivalence tests)
265   does not depend on the model and can be applied to such formulations as well.

266   3.1. Wind Model and Direction Discretization
267       For each direction, the wind speed s has the Weibull density ps (s; ζ, ψ) = (ζ/ψ)
268   (s/ψ)ζ−1 exp[−(s/ψ)ζ ] with shape ζ(θ) and scale ψ(θ). The benchmark rose is piecewise
269   constant: Nθ = 24 direction bins [15(l − 1), 15l)◦ , l = 1, . . . , 24, with probability pl , shape
270   ζ = 2 and scale ψl (Table S1). Data Set I is nearly unidirectional (ψ = 13 m/s in all
271   bins; 80% of the probability in bins 6 and 7, i.e., wind from 165◦ to 195◦ ); Data Set II is
272   a broad rose with scales from 2.6 to 10 m/s and its highest probability in bin 13. Both
273   are synthetic wind roses of the Kusiak–Song benchmark [3, 35], not measurements at a
274   site; the only measured wind climate in this study is the 12-sector Weibull rose of the
275   Horns Rev 1 block (Section 9.3), evaluated in 5◦ bins, and IEA37 Case Study 1 uses 16
276   directions at one speed (Section 6.3).
277       Why the direction discretization matters. The objective evaluates the wake only at
278   the 24 bin centers θ̄l = (15l − 7.5)◦ . A top-hat wake is narrow: by the half-cone geometry
279   (Fig. 1c), turbine j wakes a turbine i at√ the distance ρ ≥ R/K only for directions within
280   an interval of width 2[α + arcsin(R/(ρ 1 + K 2 ))] around the direction from j to i. With
281   K = 0.075 and R = 38.5 m (α = 4.29◦ , R/K = 513 m), this width is below the bin
282   spacing of 15◦ for ρ > 685 m and tends to 2α = 8.6◦ . If no bin center lies in this interval,
283   the wake of the pair does not enter the objective (with bins of 5◦ or finer, every such
284   interval contains a center). Optimized layouts exploit these gaps: with 1◦ sub-bins that
285   keep the Weibull parameters of their 15◦ bin, the mean wake loss of PSO-VNS rises from
286   1.322% to 2.335%, and the rise is largest for PSO and the VNS-based methods. All results
287   thus hold for the discretized roses; Section 10 reports which conclusions survive a 1◦ rose.

472   5.1. Two-Phase Template
473       All hybrids follow one sequential (relay) template. For a budget of B evaluations and
474   a split ω ∈ (0, 1), Phase 1 starts from the seeded initial population, runs a population

476   (Algorithm 2), which applies the local search to it and then iterates until B evaluations
477   are used. Only Phase 1 differs: PSO in PSO-VNS ; SSA or LX-SSA in SSA-VNS and LX-

599    (a) Seed level (the fixed benchmark). Estimand: the benchmark average of the expected
600        case-mean differences of the given benchmark cases; only the run-to-run variability is
601        random. Interval: percentile bootstrap that resamples the 30 seed-paired runs within

603        runs).
604    (b) Case level (exchangeable cases). Estimand: the expected case-mean difference over a
605        population of cases like the 68; the cases are treated as independent draws. Interval:
606        percentile bootstrap over the cases.
607    (c) Cluster level (new farms). Estimand: the expected difference over new (data set,
608        radius) clusters; the 68 cases form six clusters with nested N . Interval: cluster-
609        robust t interval with the bias-reduced CR2 variance and a t distribution with G−1 =
610        5 degrees of freedom (G = 6 clusters), and a restricted wild-cluster bootstrap-t TOST
611        (CR2-studentized, all 66 draws of Webb’s six-point weights enumerated); equivalence
612        at this level requires both.
613   The minimal margin of a level, mmin = max(|ℓ|, |u|) for the 90% interval [ℓ, u], is the
614   smallest margin at which equivalence holds: it holds for every m > mmin and for none
615   smaller.

616       TOST has size at most α without a multiplicity correction (intersection–union prin-
617   ciple; Section S6.4). The estimate ∆L is the same at every level, but the interval widens
618   from the seed to the cluster level, so mmin grows and an equivalence statement must name
619   its level (Section 7.3). “A gives no gain larger than m over B” (non-superiority) needs
620   only one of the two tests: the lower end of the 90% interval must lie above −m. A re-
621   sult that is neither equivalent nor significantly different is inconclusive and supports no
622   statement of the kind “as good as”. The margin m = 0.05 pp (4.1 and 2.1 MWh/yr per
623   turbine in Data Sets I and II) is a post hoc convention; the minimal margins mmin let
624   readers apply their own.

628   (dc + dc′ )/2 below −m, inside the region of practical equivalence (ROPE) [−m, m] and

821   8.1. The VNS Phase
822       The VNS phase improves both salp-swarm methods: SSA-VNS is 39/29/0 (W/T/L)
823   against SSA and LX-SSA-VNS 34/34/0 against LX-SSA (∆L = −0.294 and −0.394). For
824   PSO, which PSO-VNS repeats for its first 3,030 evaluations (Section 5), the difference is
825   small, not significant and practically equivalent to zero (∆L = −0.018 [−0.041, 0.004],
826   p = 0.59). At this budget the VNS phase is essentially one compass-search descent: for
827   PSO-VNS, the shake-and-search cycles take 21.5% of the Phase-2 evaluations (pooled;
828   median run 4.7%) but give 0.5% of the Phase-2 gain (2.5% with the cycle cut off by the
829   budget), and the median run with N ≥ 12 never shakes (Table S50). The analysis thus
830   measures the value of a local-search phase.
831       In Table S50 (12 split cases × 10 seeds), the first descent from the switch point takes
832   a median of 2,860 evaluations in PSO-VNS and gives 97.5% of the Phase-2 improvement
833   (SSA-VNS 90.3%, RS-VNS 88.6%). A shake costs one evaluation, but the new descent
834   after it scans 2n = 4N moves per step, and 114 of 120 runs accept no cycle; accepted
835   cycles use only k = 1. For N ≥ 12 the first descent is unfinished in 39 of 40 runs, so the
836   local-minimality guarantee (Proposition S9) does not apply there. VNS never makes a
837   feasible incumbent infeasible and starts from the best feasible Phase-1 layout when one
838   exists. The contrasts thus test a truncated compass search after Phase 1; shaking and
839   neighborhood change can matter only for small N .

840   8.2. Random-Sampling Controls
841       Random sampling of equal cost replaces Phase 1, uniform in the site disc (RSD-VNS)
842   or in its bounding square (RS-VNS); VNS alone is a third control. RS-VNS is weak: 730
843   of its runs draw no feasible sample, although all of them draw samples wholly inside the

1029   10. Sensitivity to the Modeling Assumptions
1030       The benchmark fixes a power curve, a wake model, a minimum spacing, a boundary
1031   rule and a discretization of the wind rose. We re-evaluated every feasible final layout of
1032   the main comparison, without re-optimization, under a cubic power curve, a Gaussian
1033   wake and finer direction bins (Table 12; all methods: Table S36); this asks whether the
1034   delivered layouts keep their order under another model, not which layouts the methods
1035   would find for it.

1036   10.1. Power Curve
1037      A cubic power curve between cut-in and rated speed [80] (also with a 25 m/s cut-
1038   out) lowers the expected power by 17.7% (Data Set I) and 36.9% (Data Set II), so our
1039   energy values are benchmark values, but it leaves the ranking intact (τ̄ = 0.95; same best
1040   method in 94% of the cases; PSO-VNS first, also with the cut-out). This check concerns


                                                                 33
1041   the rankings: the ±0.05 pp equivalence verdicts (Sections 7.3 and 8) were evaluated under
1042   the linear benchmark curve only.

1043   10.2. Wake Model
1044       The uncalibrated Gaussian wake of [48] (KG = 0.04; Section S5.1) differs from the
1045   Jensen cone (K = 0.075) in its smooth lateral profile and expansion rate and, unlike the
1046   cone test of the benchmark, gives no deficit to turbines upstream of the wake source.
1047   The mean objective changes little (Table 12), but the wake loss of the same final layouts
1048   differs between the two models by 0.38 pp on average (median 0.27 pp), 7.6 times the
1049   equivalence margin (Section 6.2), and by more than the margin for 86% of the 15,614
1050   layouts, whereas the per-case difference PSO-VNS − PSO changes by only 0.031 pp on
1051   average. The ordering is less stable (τ̄ = 0.66), and PSO is narrowly first (1.91 vs. 1.97);
1052   with 1◦ direction bins, PSO-VNS is first under the Gaussian wake as well (Section 10.5).

1053   10.3. Minimum Spacing
1054       The optimized layouts often lie on the 4D constraint: 49% of the feasible final layouts
1055   also satisfy 5D and 36% 6D (4.1% and 0.1% for N = 11–15, where the fixed site leaves less
1056   room), so they do not transfer to larger spacings without re-optimization (site capacity:
1057   Table S37). Because the benchmark fixes ℓmin = 4D, the methods of the main comparison,
1058   PSO-VNS and PSO included, were not re-run at 5D or 6D; only archived SSA and LX-
1059   SSA re-optimizations exist at these spacings (Table S38). Whether the ranking holds at
1060   larger spacings is therefore open (Section 12).

1061   10.4. Boundary Rule
1062       All methods clip the coordinates to the bounding square and leave the circle to the
1063   penalty (Section 3.4). The rule matters: an SSA that projects turbines radially onto the
1064   circle instead attains higher mean objectives than the original SSA in all six representative
1065   cases (p ≤ 0.0036), as the projection returns a turbine to the site directly. All methods
1066   therefore use the same rule: the comparison is one of search strategies under a common
1067   repair.

1068   10.5. Direction Resolution
1069       The benchmark evaluates the wake only at the centers of 24 direction bins of 15◦ , so a
1070   pair of turbines can be placed with no bin center in its narrow wake interval (Section 3).
1071   We split each bin into 3 (5◦ ) or 15 (1◦ ) sub-bins with its Weibull parameters and an
1072   equal share of its frequency (wake-free power unchanged) and re-evaluated all 21,716
1073   feasible final layouts (Table S39). The 1◦ rose raises the mean wake loss of every method,
1074   by 0.40 pp (DE) to 1.04 pp (PSO; PSO-VNS: 1.322% to 2.335%), mostly already with
1075   5◦ sub-bins: optimized layouts exploit the gaps between the coarse bins, those of PSO
1076   and the VNS-based methods most. PSO-VNS still ranks first (2.09; PSO 2.37, the only
1077   method not significantly different), also under the Gaussian wake at 1◦ (2.22 vs. 2.35);
1078   PSO’s narrow lead under the Gaussian wake with 15◦ bins (1.91 vs. 1.97; τ̄ = 0.66) is
1079   a property of the coarse rose. Beyond the top two the order changes (τ̄ = 0.52; same
1080   best method in 37% of the cases): MS-SLSQP moves from sixth to third, and its gap to
1081   PSO-VNS shrinks from 0.435 to 0.135 pp but remains significant.
1082       For PSO-VNS vs. PSO (Table S39; details: Table S40), the case-mean difference
1083   becomes −0.043 pp (90% CI [−0.062, −0.025]): a small but significant advantage (p =
1084   2.5 × 10−4 ), not equivalence at ±0.05 pp (minimal margin 0.062 pp); the same holds with


                                                    34
1085   5◦ sub-bins. The sign of the per-case difference changes in 26 of the 60 cases with a
1086   nonzero difference, so the per-case winner often depends on the discretization. On the 44
1087   non-trivial cases, equivalence is shown at neither resolution, and the Data Set II N ≥ 10
1088   advantage remains (10 of 10 cases). The component-analysis conclusions hold, but the
1089   small SSA gain over RS-VNS is no longer significant (Table S41). The order beyond the
1090   top two and the equivalence verdict are thus properties of the discretized benchmark; the
1091   first place of PSO-VNS is not.

1092   11. Discussion
1093       In this benchmark, which method appears best depends strongly on the protocol; we
1094   discuss why, what follows for hybrid design and layout practice, and condense the protocol
1095   into a checklist.

1096   11.1. What Drives the Conclusions of Metaheuristic Comparisons
1097       Baseline configuration. An advantage over PSO is informative only if the PSO base-
1098   line is convergent: the old setting lies beyond the order-2 boundary (11), which marks
1099   approximately where this PSO starts to fail, and places PSO at position 5 of Table 3
1100   instead of 1 (Section 7.1). The c-sweep links this to Proposition 1 without establishing a
1101   cause (feasibility starts to decline in the step containing c∗ = 1.7486), and velocity clamp-
1102   ing largely restores feasibility (92.2%), whereas zeroing clipped components does not: the
1103   setting fails mainly through large steps. The stability region is thus a cheap screen for
1104   settings, not a guarantee.
1105       Controls. An advantage of a hybrid’s first phase is informative only if it beats random
1106   sampling in the farm at equal cost. Square sampling is too weak a control (730 RS-VNS
1107   runs draw no feasible sample, mainly because of the spacing), so a salp-swarm phase
1108   appears to help against it; against disc sampling only the PSO phase gains, and the small
1109   SSA gain over RS-VNS is no longer significant with 1◦ bins (Section 8). With essentially
1110   one compass-search descent as second phase, this measures the value of a start [38].
1111       Level of equivalence. Non-significance is not equivalence, and equivalence depends on
1112   the level and the evaluation: PSO-VNS and PSO are equivalent at ±0.05 pp across seeds
1113   and cases but not across farm clusters, nor under a 1◦ rose, where PSO-VNS has a small
1114   significant advantage (Sections 7.3 and 10). The levels answer different questions (this
1115   benchmark, similar cases, new farms), and the minimal margin grows with them (0.026,
1116   0.041 and 0.067 pp); equivalence holds for N < 10 but not for N ≥ 10, and 19 of 68 cases
1117   differ by more than the margin, in both directions.
1118       Discretization, initialization and budget. With 1◦ sub-bins the wake loss of every
1119   method rises (PSO-VNS: 1.322% to 2.335%), as optimized layouts exploit the gaps be-
1120   tween the 15◦ bins (Section 10). PSO-VNS and PSO keep the first two places, but
1121   MS-SLSQP moves from sixth to third and PSO’s lead under the Gaussian wake with 15◦
1122   bins disappears. From feasible starts, a single local search (VNS alone) ranks first on the
1123   six largest cases (Section 9.2), and at 120,030 evaluations the leading methods lie within
1124   0.17 pp (Section 9.1). A ranking is thus the ranking of a protocol.

1125   11.2. Implications for the Design of Two-Phase Swarm–Local-Search Hybrids
1126       The swarm phase as a feasibility-reaching start. A first phase need not be a good
1127   optimizer, only a cheap source of good starts; in our runs, the PSO phase is valuable
1128   largely because it reaches feasibility: at the switch, its global best is feasible in 98.7% of
1129   the runs, against 95.7% for SSA and 75.7% for disc sampling, whereas feasible disc samples

                                                    35
1130   are not worse (wake loss 1.53% vs. 1.63%, over different runs). From feasible starts, PSO
1131   never improves on the best initial layout and VNS alone ranks first (Section 9.2). A
1132   swarm phase is thus most useful when random layouts are infeasible; when a feasible start
1133   is cheap (our packing initialization costs no objective evaluations), the budget is better
1134   spent on the local search.
1135       The local-search phase and the split. At 6,030 evaluations, the first compass-search
1136   descent gives 97.5% of the Phase-2 gain and is unfinished in 39 of 40 runs with N ≥ 12
1137   (Section 8). At small budgets the cost of the descent (2n evaluations per step) thus
1138   matters more than the neighborhoods, which act only for small N ; cheaper descents are
1139   obvious candidates, but untested. The first descent also repairs infeasible switch points
1140   (densest cases: 63.3% feasible at the switch, all at the end). Of the splits tested, ω = 0.75
1141   was best and plain PSO (ω = 1) had a higher mean loss (Section 8.4): the swarm needs
1142   enough evaluations to reach feasibility, and the descent enough to progress.
1143       Where PSO-VNS helps and where it does not. At 6,030 evaluations from random
1144   starts, PSO-VNS reaches feasibility more reliably on the Horns Rev 1 block (30/30 vs.
1145   19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 ; on the benchmark PSO is feasible in every run
1146   as well), ranks best on the six largest cases at all three budgets (exploratory), and, in
1147   the post hoc subgroup of the larger Data Set II layouts (N ≥ 10), has the lower mean
1148   wake loss in 10 of 10 cases (pHolm ≤ 0.018), also with 1◦ bins. It does not help from
1149   feasible starts, in the densest Data Set I layouts (all 7 Data Set I cases beyond the margin
1150   favor PSO), on IEA37, or on the benchmark average by more than a few hundredths of a
1151   percentage point.

1152   11.3. Implications for Wind Farm Layout Practice
1153       The direction discretization is the main caveat for practice: with 1◦ bins the benchmark
1154   wake loss rises by 0.40–1.04 pp, and none of the 901 feasible optimized Horns Rev 1 layouts
1155   exceeds the installed block (16 with the 5◦ bins used in optimization). Gains of layouts
1156   optimized under a coarse rose should be verified at a fine resolution before they are
1157   credited to an optimizer.
1158       The Horns Rev 1 block, without the other 64 turbines, with 4D instead of 7D spacing
1159   and without cables, loads or navigation, tests optimizers, not the installed design (Sec-
1160   tion 9.3). Our model takes CT at the free-stream speed (wake loss −0.72 pp relative to
1161   PyWake’s NOJ model); with CT at the local speed it reproduces PyWake’s hub-center
1162   NOJ exactly, as design studies require. On IEA37, the best PSO-VNS layouts at 30,030
1163   evaluations lie 1.93% (16 turbines) and 6.23% (36 turbines) below the best feasible pub-
1164   lished layouts in AEP; they came from the participants’ own optimizers, some gradient-
1165   based, with budgets not reported comparably [4, 17]. The methods compared here, at
1166   these budgets, are reference points for comparing optimizers, not design tools.

1204   13. Conclusions and Future Work
1205        On the discretized Kusiak–Song benchmark with random starts and 6,030 evaluations,
1206   three findings emerge. First, the baseline configuration changes the conclusions: the old
1207   PSO setting, which lies beyond the order-2 boundary and rarely samples feasible layouts
1208   late in the run, puts PSO behind salp-swarm hybrids, whereas the constriction setting
1209   puts it ahead of every salp-swarm method at this budget. Second, salp-swarm first phases
1210   give no gain larger than the 0.05 pp margin over random sampling in the farm (a larger
1211   gain is ruled out across seeds and cases, not farm clusters; LX-SSA as published is worse),
1212   whereas a PSO phase gives the second phase, essentially one compass-search descent, a
1213   clearly better start. Third, PSO-VNS has the best average rank with 15◦ , 5◦ and 1◦
1214   direction bins, also under a Gaussian wake at 1◦ , but its average advantage over a well-
1215   configured PSO is small: within ±0.05 pp under the benchmark rose (across seeds and
1216   cases, not farm clusters) and −0.043 pp under a 1◦ rose, with case-level differences in
1217   both directions that follow a data set × density pattern (exploratory). PSO-VNS reaches
1218   feasibility more reliably on Horns Rev 1 (30/30 vs. 19/30 runs, McNemar p = 9.8 × 10−4 );
1219   it is a consistent reference method, not a superior optimizer.
1220        Future work follows from the threats above. Expensive wake models: surrogate-assisted
1221   evaluation (graph neural networks mapping a layout and a wind state to turbine powers,
1222   or data-driven and Kriging surrogates [53–55]) would make equal-budget comparisons at
1223   higher fidelity affordable, if the surrogate error is reported next to the method differences.
1224   Larger farms, such as the IEA37 64-turbine scenario and the full 80-turbine Horns Rev 1.
1225   Exact-gradient baselines on a smooth wake model [17], instead of finite differences on
1226   a discontinuous objective. Stronger baselines and controls: CMA-ES, L-SHADE, tuned
1227   DE, a cost-matched ablation of the Laplace step, and random-sampling controls at larger
1228   budgets. Finer roses during optimization, with loads and economic objectives.
