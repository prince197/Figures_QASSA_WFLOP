Table~\ref{tab:farm-summary} summarizes the nine methods over the four wind farm types. PSO-VNS ranks first in six of the nine settings and second or third in the other three, in which GA ranks first: the Horns Rev~1 block at 30,030 evaluations and both IEA37 budgets with 36 turbines. PSO-VNS ranks ahead of PSO in every setting and is significantly better than PSO in every site setting, but not on the benchmark, where the two are practically equivalent at the seed level (Section~\ref{sec:psovns-pso}). \subsection{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\label{sec:disc-where}
\label{sec:disc-practice}
We proposed PSO-VNS, a two-phase method for the WFLOP that runs the standard PSO, with the constriction coefficients of Clerc and Kennedy, for half of the evaluation budget and then a basic VNS from the best layout found. We compared it with eight methods (PSO, SSA-VNS, SSA, LX-SSA, DE, GA, VNS and MS-SLSQP) on four types of wind farm, with equal evaluation budgets, seed-paired runs, random starts and one penalty and repair for all methods. The findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Type~I, Kusiak--Song benchmark (68 cases; Section~\ref{sec:farm-I}).} PSO-VNS has the best average rank, followed by PSO and GA; it is not significantly different from these two and significantly better than the other six methods. Its average advantage over PSO is small: within the 0.05~pp margin on this fixed benchmark and under exchangeable-case sensitivity, not under scenario-cluster sensitivity. Case by case, the differences go both ways: PSO-VNS has the lower wake loss in all ten Data Set~II cases with $N\ge10$ (post hoc), whereas every Data Set~I case in which the two differ by more than the margin, all of them dense, favors PSO. On the all-run endpoint, PSO-VNS is better than PSO more often than not.

\item \emph{Type~II, Horns Rev~1 block (Section~\ref{sec:farm-II}).} PSO-VNS ranks first at 6,030 evaluations and is feasible in all 30 runs (PSO: 19); at 30,030 evaluations GA ranks first and PSO-VNS second. The block (16 of 80 turbines, $4D$ instead of $7D$ spacing, no cables, loads or navigation) tests optimizers, not the installed design: with 1$^\circ$ direction bins, no optimized layout exceeds the installed block.

\item \emph{Type~III, Lillgrund block (Section~\ref{sec:farm-III}).} PSO-VNS ranks first by mean AEP at both budgets and is feasible far more often than PSO. At 6,030 evaluations, however, MS-SLSQP is more reliable (feasible in all 30 runs, PSO-VNS in 17, PSO in none), and the AEP lead of PSO-VNS over the runners-up is not significant with 1$^\circ$ bins.
% CHECK (manual, 2026-10-04): Lillgrund 17/30 vs 0/30 and 30/30 vs 19/30; at 1 deg PSO-VNS highest mean at 30,030 but p_Holm 0.16 / 0.58 / 0.58 vs SSA-VNS / VNS / MS-SLSQP, the runners-up (tab:S-sa-lg-eval)
\item \emph{Type~IV, IEA37 Case Study~1 (Section~\ref{sec:farm-IV}).} PSO-VNS ranks first with 16 turbines and GA with 36 turbines, at both budgets. On this piecewise-smooth model, SLSQP with exact gradients, alone or after a PSO phase, does better than every metaheuristic compared, with feasibility labels as reported by the runs. For smooth, differentiable wake models, our results thus favor gradient-based optimization, possibly started from a swarm; the metaheuristic comparisons of this paper are relevant where the objective is discontinuous, as with the top-hat wake of the benchmark, or gradients are unavailable.
% CHECK-FINAL [C40]: no method reaches the best published IEA37 layouts; CHECK-DIR [F44]: projected IEA37 gaps (30,030)
\item \emph{Ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).} The PSO phase is the component that matters: it gives the second phase, essentially one compass-search descent, a clearly better start than random sampling in the farm. The salp-swarm first phases give no gain of practical size over random sampling in the farm at the benchmark spacing $4D$ (at $6D$ the SSA phase does gain), and LX-SSA as published makes them worse. After PSO, the VNS phase adds little on the benchmark.

\item \emph{Robustness (Sections~\ref{sec:robustness} and~\ref{sec:feasinit}).} On the benchmark, PSO-VNS keeps first place under a cubic power curve, when its layouts are re-evaluated with 1$^\circ$ direction bins and when the search itself uses 1$^\circ$ bins (eight cases), where its advantage over PSO exceeds the margin; under the Gaussian wake with 15$^\circ$ bins, PSO ranks narrowly first. Finer bins raise the wake loss of every method, because layouts optimized under a coarse rose exploit the gaps between its bins; gains found under a coarse rose should therefore be checked, and preferably obtained, at a fine resolution. The lead of PSO-VNS depends on random starts (from feasible starts, it is not first) and partly on constraint handling: with Deb's feasibility rules and box clipping, the order of the leading methods changes; with radial projection, it is kept, but the all-run advantage of PSO-VNS over PSO is not significant.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\label{sec:disc-recs}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Constraint handling and configuration.} All methods minimize one penalty, which adds boundary violations in m$^2$ to spacing violations in m ($\mu=10^{10}$), with box clipping; the conclusions partly depend on this choice (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}). The weaknesses observed for SSA, LX-SSA and DE therefore characterize these implementations under this penalty and repair, not their algorithm families. DE is untuned (its low feasibility depends on the repair), and MS-SLSQP uses finite differences on a discontinuous objective.
% CHECK-DIAG [D01]/[D02]: instrumented runs reproduce the stored runs; generated \NFeasDE ("depends on the repair": DE feasibility 33.3 % -> 80.0 % with Deb + projection, rev3_constraint.json); CHECK-FINAL [C17]: LX-SSA as published
\item \emph{Local search.} At 6,030 evaluations the VNS phase is essentially one truncated compass-search descent, so the ablation concerns this local search, not VNS in general.
% CHECK-DIAG [D08]-[D10]: truncated compass search
\item \emph{Model.} The benchmark uses the top-hat Jensen wake, with its non-physical upstream deficit (Section~\ref{sec:model}), and a linear power curve, so its objective is a benchmark value, not farm quality; except in the direct 1$^\circ$ study (eight cases), the search used coarse wind roses. Turbulence and loads (critical at $4D$), wake steering, blockage, terrain, cables, electrical losses and costs are outside our scope; all AEP values are gross.
% CHECK-FINAL [C21]: cubic curve lowers the power, ranking intact; CHECK-DIR [F32]/[F34]: PyWake 661.39 vs ours 666.75 GWh/yr, wake loss -0.72 pp, cause C_T at free-stream speed
\item \emph{Farms and budgets.} The benchmark farms are circular, with a prescribed $N\le15$ and $4D$ spacing; measured wind climates enter only through two 16-turbine blocks, on which the feasibility advantage of PSO-VNS rests and whose AEP rankings partly depend on the evaluator; the 64-turbine IEA37 scenario was not run. The rankings are those of small budgets: at a larger budget the order of the methods changes (Section~\ref{sec:budget}).
% CHECK-FINAL [C45] (budget study: SSA-VNS ahead of PSO at 120,030); CHECK-EXTRA [X52]: Horns Rev feasibility (one site, random starts); CHECK (manual, 2026-10-04): GA lead at 30,030 under 1-deg bins and PyWake NOJ (rev3_sites.json); Lillgrund PSO-VNS lead over the runners-up SSA-VNS, VNS, MS-SLSQP n.s. at 1 deg (p_Holm 0.16, 0.58, 0.58); still significant vs PSO, SSA, LX-SSA, DE, RSD-VNS
\item \emph{Method pool.} The pool lacks CMA-ES~\cite{HansenOstermeier2001}, L-SHADE~\cite{Tanabe2014}, adaptive GAs, ACO~\cite{Eroglu2012} and BBO~\cite{Bansal2017}, so ``first'' means first among the nine methods compared. Exact-gradient SLSQP was run only on IEA37, and 220 of its 240 strict feasibility labels cannot be decided from the stored coordinates (Section~\ref{sec:archive-reliability}; worst case: Table~\ref{S-tab:S-sa-feasworst}).
\item \emph{Statistics.} The equivalence margin and the subgroup analyses are post hoc, and equivalence statements other than the two primary questions (Section~\ref{sec:setup-stats}) are exploratory. The 68 cases form only six clusters with nested $N$, so the p-values describe this benchmark, not a population of farms. The studies on subsets of cases (budget, initialization, spacing, constraint handling and direct 1$^\circ$ optimization) are estimates for those cases, and their error rates are not controlled jointly.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Directions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Expensive wake models}: surrogate-assisted evaluation (graph neural network, data-driven or Kriging surrogates~\cite{Yang2023,Wang2024,Li2025}) would make equal-budget comparisons at higher fidelity affordable, if the surrogate error is reported next to the method differences.
\item \emph{Larger farms and more sites}, such as the IEA37 64-turbine scenario and the full Horns Rev~1 and Lillgrund farms.
\item \emph{Gradient-based methods} on smooth models of larger farms~\cite{Rodrigues2024}, and swarm--gradient hybrids, under budgets that charge every gradient.
\item \emph{Stronger reference methods and constraint handling}: CMA-ES, L-SHADE, tuned DE, and constraint handling designed for each method family.
\item \emph{Fine wind roses during optimization} on all cases, with loads and economic objectives.
\end{enumerate}
