\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Where the turbines of a wind farm are placed is decided once, at the planning stage, and this decision determines how much of the available wind energy the farm converts over its entire lifetime. An upstream turbine leaves behind a wake of slower and more turbulent air that reduces the power of the turbines downstream, so the layout sets the wake loss and thereby the gross energy yield, and shapes the collector system and grid connection~\cite{Srikakulapu2018,Hou2019}. As the wake loss is a share of the wake-free energy, a difference of a few tenths of a percentage point is, within a given wake model, a lifetime energy difference of that size. The wind farm layout optimization problem (WFLOP) places a given number of turbines in a site to minimize the wake loss subject to boundary and minimum-spacing constraints; we address only this wake-loss part of layout design, for farms of up to 36 turbines.

For swarm and evolutionary computation, the continuous WFLOP is also a representative constrained benchmark. Its $2N$ coordinates are continuous, the objective is multimodal and, with a top-hat wake, discontinuous; the constraints are a site boundary and $N(N-1)/2$ pairwise spacing constraints, so the feasible region is nonconvex and becomes a small part of the bounding box as $N$ grows; one evaluation is cheap enough for thousands of runs; and standard instances with published results exist, from the Kusiak--Song cases~\cite{Kusiak2010} to the IEA Wind Task~37 case studies~\cite{Baker2019}. These properties, which it shares with many engineering design problems, have made it a common test bed for new metaheuristics~\cite{Azlan2021}, including swarm methods such as particle swarm optimization (PSO)~\cite{Pookpunt2013,Asaah2021} and, more recently, metaphor-based algorithms such as the salp swarm algorithm (SSA)~\cite{Mirjalili2017,Rezk2019}. What such studies can show depends on how the comparison is run.

For the continuous WFLOP, a single method rarely does well in both of the tasks the problem poses. Reaching the feasible region from random layouts calls for a population that moves whole layouts at once, whereas the last improvements are small moves of individual turbines, which a contracted swarm makes only by chance; and the top-hat wake makes the objective discontinuous, so that gradients cannot be used for this refinement. PSO is a well-understood global search with few parameters, and VNS a derivative-free local search with few parameters; combining them has helped in scheduling, routing and localization~\cite{Tasgetiren2007,Marinakis2017,Gumaida2019}, but the embedded designs used there spend local-search evaluations in every iteration and have not, to our knowledge, been applied to the WFLOP. This motivates a simple sequential variant whose phases have fixed, exactly counted budgets and whose contribution can be measured (Section~\ref{sec:psovns-motivation}).

A new algorithm, including the one proposed here, is justified by a comparison with other methods, and its claimed advantage is only as reliable as that comparison. Guidelines for experiments with metaheuristics~\cite{Hooker1995,Derrac2011,BartzBeielstein2020,LaTorre2021} and critiques of metaphor-based algorithms~\cite{Sorensen2015,Aranha2022} both point to the same weak spots: baselines run with poor or unreported settings, budgets that are not equal, constraint violations that are ignored in the ranking, and statistics that confuse the absence of a significant difference with equivalence. In the WFLOP, the GECCO layout competition~\cite{Wilson2018} and the IEA Wind Task~37 studies~\cite{Baker2019,Thomas2023} compared optimizers on a common objective, and reviews list many metaheuristics tested under differing budgets and settings~\cite{Azlan2021}; these studies compare methods as submitted or as published, not under one protocol (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Three issues matter here. First, budgets counted in iterations, or without the gradient evaluations of gradient-based solvers, are not equal. Second, infeasible layouts returned by penalty-based metaheuristics must be ranked consistently. Third, mean values without paired tests and multiplicity correction~\cite{Benavoli2016} may mislead, and a nonsignificant difference is not evidence of equivalence~\cite{Lakens2017}. For two-phase hybrids, a further question is whether the first phase contributes more than a random start would.

In this paper, we propose PSO-VNS, a new two-phase variant of PSO with the basic variable neighborhood search (VNS)~\cite{Mladenovic1997,Hansen2001}, a hybrid in the memetic sense~\cite{Neri2012}, for the continuous WFLOP. PSO-VNS runs the standard constriction PSO and then refines its best layout by VNS. We compare it with eight methods: PSO, the SSA with VNS (SSA-VNS), the SSA, the Laplacian SSA (LX-SSA), previously proposed by Solanki and Deep~\cite{Solanki2023}, differential evolution (DE), a genetic algorithm (GA), VNS alone and multistart sequential least-squares programming (MS-SLSQP). The comparison covers four wind farm types: Type~I, the discretized Kusiak--Song benchmark, with two wind data sets, one with the same wind-speed distribution in all directions and one with direction-dependent wind speeds, on circular farms of three sizes (68 cases); Type~II, a 16-turbine block of Horns Rev~1; Type~III, a $4\times4$ block of Lillgrund; and Type~IV, IEA37 Case Study~1 with 16 and 36 turbines. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Algorithm.} PSO-VNS runs a constriction PSO for a share $\omega$ of the budget and the basic VNS from its best layout for the rest (Section~\ref{sec:psovns-method}). The sequential design uses the swarm to reach the feasible region and the local search to refine one layout, gives both phases exactly counted budgets, adds a single parameter to those of PSO and VNS and, as its first phase reproduces a PSO run exactly, lets the gain of the local search be measured against PSO with the same seeds.
\item \emph{Evaluation.} The nine methods are run on the four wind farm types under one protocol, following established practice~\cite{Derrac2011,BartzBeielstein2020,LaTorre2021}: equal budgets in objective evaluations, counting the gradient evaluations of MS-SLSQP; seed-paired runs; a feasibility-aware ranking with an all-run endpoint, as in constrained evolutionary computation~\cite{Deb2000,Wu2017}; Friedman and Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; and an equivalence analysis~\cite{Lakens2017,Benavoli2017} with a post hoc margin of $\pm0.05$ percentage points (pp) of wake loss, decided at the seed level of the fixed benchmark, with case and cluster levels as sensitivity analyses (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The individual elements are established; their joint use we have not found reported in WFLOP comparisons. All results are regenerated from a reproducibility package.

\item \emph{Performance of PSO-VNS by wind farm type.} On Type~I, PSO-VNS has the best average rank of the nine methods, followed by PSO and GA; it is not significantly different from these two and significantly better than the other six. Its mean difference to PSO lies within the margin at the seed and case levels, not at the scenario-cluster level, with case-level differences in both directions. On Horns Rev~1 (Type~II), PSO-VNS ranks first at 6,030 evaluations and GA at 30,030. On Lillgrund (Type~III), PSO-VNS ranks first at both budgets, although at 6,030 evaluations MS-SLSQP, feasible in all runs, is better in the paired test. On IEA37 (Type~IV), PSO-VNS ranks first with 16 turbines and GA with 36 (Section~\ref{sec:results}). When the search itself uses 1$^\circ$ direction bins (eight cases of Type~I), PSO-VNS stays first and its advantage over PSO, about 0.19~pp, exceeds the margin (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). As the PSO phase of PSO-VNS reproduces a PSO run exactly for a given seed, the comparison with PSO isolates the contribution of the local-search phase.
